Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves

Originally by One Thousand and One Nights — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 13 min read 📖 2,274 words 🌙 Best read aloud

Ali Baba was poor, and his brother Cassim was not, and this was the central fact of both their lives.

They'd grown up in the same house, eaten the same food, been taught by the same father — a merchant who had been moderately successful in the way that merchants in the old city were moderately successful, which meant he had enough to live and not enough to leave. When he died, whatever he left was divided between them, and Cassim, who was older and shrewder and understood that the world rewards initiative more than fairness, married the daughter of a wealthy merchant and absorbed her family's business the way rivers absorb tributaries — naturally, inevitably, without apology.

Ali Baba married for love.

This is presented in the original story as a noble choice, and it is, but noble choices have material consequences, and the material consequence of marrying for love rather than advantage was that Ali Baba spent the next twenty years as a woodcutter. He went to the forest each day with his three donkeys — Patience, Hope, and a third one he never named because naming all your donkeys felt like it crossed a line — and he chopped wood and loaded the donkeys and brought it to market and earned enough.

Enough. That word again. The word that can mean freedom or limitation depending on who's saying it and whether they're lying.

Ali Baba's wife was named Fatima, and she was the reason enough felt like freedom. She kept their small house the way a good captain keeps a small ship — tight, clean, everything in its place, nothing wasted. She could make a meal from what other women threw away. She could make a room feel warm when the fire was low. She was the infrastructure that made Ali Baba's modesty functional, and like most infrastructure, she was invisible to everyone except the person who depended on her completely.

He was in the forest when he heard the horses.

Not the usual sounds — the market traffic, the carts, the ordinary percussion of commerce moving through the world. These were fast horses, ridden hard, the sound of people arriving somewhere with purpose. Ali Baba did what woodcutters do when they hear fast horses in a forest: he climbed a tree.

Forty men. He counted them from the branches, pressed flat against the bark, breathing through his teeth. Forty men on forty horses, dressed in the way that men dress when they don't want to be recognized, which is the first sign that recognition would be dangerous. They stopped at the base of a rock face — a sheer wall of stone, unremarkable, the kind of geological feature you'd walk past a thousand times without looking twice.

The leader dismounted. He was tall, bearded, with the particular stillness of a man who has decided that the world belongs to him and is simply collecting it, one theft at a time. He walked to the rock face and said, clearly and without ceremony:

"Open sesame."

The rock opened.

Not metaphorically. The stone face split like a curtain parting, revealing a passage — dark, deep, leading into the mountain. The forty men rode in. The rock closed behind them.

Ali Baba waited. A long time — long enough for the sweat on his back to dry, long enough for his heartbeat to slow from a sprint to a walk, long enough to be certain they were gone. Then he climbed down.

He stood before the rock.

He should have left. Every reasonable impulse in him said leave, go home, forget what you saw, this is not your business and not your world and the gap between woodcutting and whatever this is could kill you. Reason said leave. Curiosity said stay. And curiosity, in the moment, was louder.

"Open sesame," he said.

The rock opened.

Inside: treasure. Not a chest of coins or a pile of gems — the cartoonish image of treasure that children imagine. This was the accumulated wealth of decades of organized theft: bolts of silk from the eastern caravans, spice by the barrel, gold in ingots and coins and chains and vessels, carpets so fine they seemed like liquid, weapons inlaid with silver, books bound in leather and stamped with gold. Room after room, passage after passage, the mountain hollow with stolen abundance.

Ali Baba was not a greedy man. He was a man who had lived on enough for twenty years, and the distance between enough and this was so vast that it produced not greed but vertigo. He stood in the treasure cave and felt the ground tilt beneath him, the world reshuffling its possibilities like a deck of cards.

He took gold. Not all of it — a fraction, a slice, enough to fill the saddlebags of his three donkeys and cover them with firewood. He took enough to change his life but not so much that the absence would be noticed, which is the arithmetic of someone who understands that the most dangerous form of theft is the kind that gets you caught.

He went home. He told Fatima. Fatima, who had spent twenty years building a life on discipline and restraint, looked at the gold and felt two things: relief and dread. Relief because the pressure was finally off. Dread because pressure, paradoxically, had been the organizing principle of their lives, and without it, the structure might collapse.

"We tell no one," she said.

"No one."

"Especially not Cassim."

"Especially not Cassim."

They told Cassim.

Not on purpose. But gold changes behavior the way alcohol changes behavior — subtly at first, then obviously. Ali Baba paid off debts. Bought new clothes. Fixed the roof. The improvements were modest, but modesty is relative, and to Cassim's wife — who had the particular radar for other people's prosperity that comes from defining yourself by comparison — the changes were deafening.

She sent Cassim to investigate. Cassim, who had spent his life being the successful brother, who had built his identity on the gap between his wealth and Ali Baba's poverty, who needed that gap the way some people need a mirror — to see himself clearly, to confirm he was ahead.

Cassim confronted Ali Baba. Ali Baba, who was a poor liar because he was a poor liar, told him everything.

Cassim went to the cave. He went with ten mules and empty saddlebags and the particular frenzy of a man who sees an opportunity and grabs it with both hands without checking what he's grabbing.

"Open sesame."

The rock opened. Cassim went in. He filled every bag, every saddlebag, every container he could find, taking not a fraction but a fortune, loading the mules until they groaned.

He went to leave.

He couldn't remember the words.

"Open barley. Open wheat. Open millet."

The rock stayed closed. The words that had seemed so simple — two syllables, a child could remember them — were gone, buried under the panic that was rising in Cassim's chest like water in a flooding room. He tried every grain he could think of, every seed, every crop, stumbling through the agricultural vocabulary of the region with increasing desperation.

The thieves returned.

They found Cassim in the cave, surrounded by their gold, sweating, wild-eyed, his mouth still forming the wrong words. They didn't ask questions. Thieves don't negotiate with people found inside their vault.

Ali Baba found his brother's body outside the cave, arranged as a warning — the universal language of don't come back. He brought Cassim home. He mourned him. He mourned him genuinely, despite everything, because Cassim was his brother, and brothers are mourned even when — especially when — they were difficult.

The thieves traced the theft to Ali Baba. Of course they did. Thieves are methodical — you don't run a forty-person operation without process, without investigation, without the organized pursuit of loose ends.

They came for him. Three times.

The first thief came disguised as a merchant, scouting Ali Baba's house, marking the door with chalk — this one, here, this is the house. But Morgiana saw the mark.

Morgiana. The servant. The most important person in this story who is given the least amount of billing. She was young, sharp, and possessed of the particular intelligence that servants develop — the intelligence of observation, of patterns, of reading rooms and faces and chalk marks on doors and understanding what they mean before anyone explains.

She marked every door on the street with the same chalk. The thief returned with his crew, found thirty identical marks, and went home with nothing.

The second attempt: a thief disguised as an oil merchant, forty jars in a cart, thirty-nine containing men and one containing oil. The plan was to wait until dark, emerge from the jars, kill Ali Baba in his sleep.

Morgiana discovered them. She went jar to jar in the courtyard, and instead of oil she heard breathing — the quiet, controlled breathing of men trying to be quiet, which is somehow louder than regular breathing because it contains intention.

She boiled oil. The real oil, from the real jar. And she poured it into the other thirty-nine, one by one, with the calm efficiency of a woman performing a task she finds distasteful but necessary. The story tells it quickly. The reality was slower, and hotter, and the sounds were something Morgiana never talked about afterward.

The third attempt was the leader himself. He came to dinner — invited, disguised as a merchant, introduced to Ali Baba by a mutual acquaintance. He sat at the table and ate the food and smiled the smile of a man who is counting down to the moment the knife comes out.

Morgiana recognized him. Not his face, which was disguised, but his hands. The way he held his cup. The particular stillness that she'd seen before, in the forest clearing, from the window, when the forty men had ridden in. Stillness is harder to disguise than a face.

She danced. The story says she danced for the guest — a traditional dance, performed with a dagger, a common entertainment. But what she actually did was closer to a conversation conducted in movement and metal: I know who you are. I know why you're here. And this dagger is not decorative.

She killed him. Mid-dance, mid-dinner, the blade finding its mark with the precision of someone who has been watching and waiting and planning for exactly this moment. The other guests were horrified. Ali Baba was horrified. Morgiana was not horrified, because horror is a luxury of people who have the option of doing nothing, and Morgiana had never had that option.

Ali Baba offered Morgiana her freedom. She'd been a servant — enslaved, technically, though the story uses softer words because stories always use softer words for the things that are hardest to say. He offered her freedom, and she took it, and he offered her something else: his son's hand in marriage, which was not a reward but a recognition — the acknowledgment that the person who had saved his life three times was not a servant but a partner, and always had been.

They lived well. The cave's wealth lasted — not because they were greedy but because they were careful, taking what they needed, when they needed it, and no more. The forty thieves were gone. The danger was gone. What remained was a family and a fortune and the daily, unglamorous work of living in a world where good luck and bad luck sit side by side and the only thing between them is the sharpness of the people you trust.

Here's what the story is about, underneath the cave and the gold and the magic words:

It's about Morgiana.

The woman who saw the chalk and understood it. The woman who checked the jars. The woman who recognized the hands. The woman who danced with a dagger and used it, because the men around her — kind men, decent men, men she loved — were not equipped for what was coming, and someone had to be.

Every household has a Morgiana. Every family, every business, every organization has someone who sees the chalk marks, who checks the jars, who recognizes danger by its hands rather than its face. They are rarely the protagonist. They are rarely given the billing. They work in the background, the margins, the kitchen — and when the crisis comes, they are the ones who move.

Ali Baba was lucky. Ali Baba was kind. Ali Baba found a cave full of gold and had the good sense to take only what he needed.

But Morgiana is the reason he's alive to tell the story.

Goodnight to Ali Baba, who found a fortune and had the sense to take only enough.

Goodnight to Cassim, who took too much and forgot the words. The exit is always simpler than we make it.

Goodnight to Morgiana, the servant who saved everyone and asked for nothing. The sharpest person in the room is rarely the loudest.

And goodnight to you. If you have a Morgiana in your life — someone who sees the things you miss, who checks the jars you walk past, who moves when you're still deciding — tell them. Tomorrow. In the morning. Before the thieves come.

Open sesame.

Remember the words. They're simpler than you think.

Goodnight.

Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves

Originally by One Thousand and One Nights — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 13 min read 📖 821 words 🌙 Best read aloud

Ali Baba was poor and his brother Cassim was not. Central fact of both their lives.

Same house growing up. Same father — a moderately successful merchant. When he died, Cassim married the daughter of a wealthy merchant and absorbed her family's business. Ali Baba married for love.

Noble choice. Material consequence: twenty years as a woodcutter. Three donkeys, forest every day, chop and carry and sell. Enough.

His wife Fatima was the reason enough felt like freedom. She kept their house the way a captain keeps a small ship — tight, nothing wasted. She could make a meal from what others threw away. She was the infrastructure, and like most infrastructure, invisible to everyone except the person who depended on her completely.

He was in the forest when he heard fast horses. Forty men. He climbed a tree, pressed flat. They stopped at a rock face — sheer, unremarkable.

The leader dismounted. Tall, bearded, still.

"Open sesame."

The rock split open. A passage into the mountain. Forty men rode in. The rock closed.

Ali Baba waited a long time. Then climbed down.

He should have left. Every reasonable impulse said go home, forget this. Curiosity was louder.

"Open sesame."

Inside: treasure. Not a chest of coins — decades of organized theft. Silk, spice, gold in ingots, carpets, weapons. Room after room.

He took gold. Not all — a fraction, enough for three donkey-loads, covered with firewood. Enough to change his life but not so much the absence would be noticed.

Fatima looked at the gold and felt two things: relief and dread. Relief because the pressure was off. Dread because pressure had been the organizing principle of their lives.

"We tell no one."

"No one."

"Especially not Cassim."

They told Cassim. Not on purpose. Gold changes behavior. Ali Baba paid debts, fixed the roof, bought new clothes. Cassim's wife had radar for other people's prosperity.

Cassim confronted Ali Baba. Ali Baba was a terrible liar. Told him everything.

Cassim went with ten mules and empty bags. Filled every one, taking not a fraction but a fortune.

He went to leave. Couldn't remember the words.

"Open barley. Open wheat. Open millet."

The rock stayed shut. Panic buried the simple phrase. The thieves returned. Found him in their vault.

Ali Baba found his brother's body outside the cave. A warning. He brought Cassim home. Mourned him genuinely — he was his brother, and brothers are mourned even when they're difficult.

The thieves traced the theft to Ali Baba. Came for him three times.

The first thief marked his door with chalk. But Morgiana saw it.

Morgiana. The servant. The most important person in this story. Young, sharp, with the intelligence servants develop — observation, patterns, reading chalk marks on doors.

She marked every door on the street the same way. The thief returned, found thirty identical marks, went home empty.

Second attempt: a fake oil merchant, forty jars in a cart. Thirty-nine containing men, one containing oil. Wait till dark, emerge, kill Ali Baba.

Morgiana checked the jars. Heard breathing instead of oil. Boiled the real oil. Poured it into the thirty-nine. The story tells it quickly. The reality was slower.

Third attempt: the leader himself, disguised as a dinner guest. Morgiana recognized him — not his face, which was disguised, but his hands. Stillness is harder to disguise than a face.

She danced. The traditional dance with a dagger. What she actually did was a conversation in movement and metal: I know who you are.

She killed him. Mid-dance, mid-dinner. The other guests were horrified. Morgiana was not, because horror is a luxury of people who can do nothing, and she'd never had that option.

Ali Baba offered her freedom and his son's hand in marriage — not a reward but a recognition.

They lived well. The cave's wealth lasted because they were careful. Taking what they needed, no more.

It's about Morgiana. The woman who saw the chalk. Who checked the jars. Who recognized the hands. Who danced with a dagger because the men around her — kind, decent men she loved — weren't equipped for what was coming.

Every family has a Morgiana. The one who sees what others miss. Rarely the protagonist. Working in the background. And when the crisis comes, the one who moves.

Ali Baba found the gold. But Morgiana is the reason he's alive to tell the story.

Goodnight to Ali Baba, who took only enough.

Goodnight to Cassim, who took too much and forgot the words.

Goodnight to Morgiana — the sharpest person in the room, never the loudest. If you have one in your life, tell them. Tomorrow. Before the thieves come.

Open sesame. The words are simpler than you think.

Goodnight.

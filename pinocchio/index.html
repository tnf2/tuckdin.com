Pinocchio

Originally by Carlo Collodi — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 16 min read 📖 3,280 words 🌙 Best read aloud

Geppetto carved the boy because he was lonely, and loneliness makes people build things.

Not the productive kind of building — not bridges or businesses or the useful structures that earn their place in the world through function. The other kind. The midnight kind. The I need something to talk to in this house and a cat isn't enough kind. The kind that happens in workshops at two in the morning when the tools are warm and the wood is patient and the silence has become too loud to bear.

Geppetto was old. Not elderly — he was perhaps sixty, which is old enough to have buried most of your plans and young enough to feel the absence of them. He lived alone in a village in Tuscany, in a workshop that smelled like pine shavings and linseed oil and the particular loneliness of a man who is good with his hands but has no one to hand things to.

He'd never married. Not for lack of trying — he'd been in love once, in his twenties, with a woman named Rosella who had laughed like music and left like weather, suddenly and completely, for a city he couldn't follow her to because his workshop was here and his tools were here and he was, in the way that certain men are, rooted so deeply to a place that moving would have killed the thing inside him that made him worth knowing.

He made toys. Puppets, mostly — marionettes with jointed limbs and painted faces and strings that made them dance, walk, wave, bow. They were beautiful. Everyone said so. Children pressed their faces to his window. Parents commissioned them for birthdays and holidays and the particular occasions that require a gift with soul.

But the puppets went home with other people. Geppetto's workshop stayed empty.

So, one night, he carved a boy.

He started with a piece of pine — a good piece, straight-grained, free of knots, the kind of wood that practically tells you what it wants to be. He roughed the shape with a chisel. He refined it with smaller tools, working by candlelight, the shadows jumping on the walls as the form emerged from the block: a head, a torso, arms, legs, feet. A face — round and young, with a nose that was perhaps a bit too long, but noses can be sanded down, and Geppetto was a perfectionist in all things except the things that mattered.

He painted the eyes. Blue, because Rosella's eyes had been blue, and some things you carry with you into everything you make whether you intend to or not.

He set down his tools. He looked at the puppet. The puppet looked back.

"Hello," said Geppetto, to the puppet, to the empty room, to the candle and the wood shavings and the silence.

"Hello," said the puppet.

Now. The story you remember has a fairy — a Blue Fairy who grants Geppetto's wish, who touches the puppet with a wand and says live and it lives. And maybe that's what happened. Or maybe what happened is simpler and stranger: maybe the loneliness and the love and the sixty years of making things with his hands had accumulated into something that was, itself, a kind of magic. Maybe when you pour enough of yourself into a thing — enough care, enough hours, enough of the quiet, desperate hope that the thing you're making will be the thing that fills the space inside you — maybe the thing wakes up.

Either way: the puppet was alive.

He moved. He blinked. He pulled at the strings that connected him to the crossbar and said, "What are these?"

"Strings," said Geppetto.

"I don't want strings."

"They help you move."

"I can move on my own."

And he could. He pulled free of the strings — snapping them, actually, with a strength that surprised them both — and he stood on the workbench, wobbly and unsteady, like a foal finding its legs, and he looked at Geppetto with blue painted eyes that were somehow, impossibly, seeing.

"I'm going to call you Pinocchio," Geppetto said.

"Why?"

"It means 'pine nut.' Because you're made of pine. And because you're small."

"I won't always be small."

This was the first thing Pinocchio said that was true. It would take him a long time to learn how to say true things consistently, but this one — this first one — was accurate. He would not always be small. He would grow, not in the way that wooden things grow (they don't) but in the way that people grow: painfully, unevenly, and mostly by making mistakes.

Geppetto sent him to school.

This was optimistic. Sending Pinocchio to school was like sending a match to a fireworks factory — the intention was education, the outcome was chaos. Pinocchio had been alive for approximately four hours. He had no experience, no context, no framework for understanding the world except the workshop and the old man and the vague, unnamed hunger inside him that wanted — wanted — wanted.

What did he want? He didn't know. That's the thing about want when you're new — it's all appetite and no direction. You want everything and nothing and something you can't name, and the wanting feels like itching, and you scratch it by moving, by going, by saying yes to whatever's in front of you.

On the way to school, he met a puppet theater.

The puppeteer was a large man named Mangiafuoco — Fire-Eater — who had a beard like a black waterfall and a voice like a door slamming and a business that ran on the simple principle of entertainment: make people laugh, charge them for it. He saw Pinocchio — a puppet that moved without strings — and he saw money.

"Come work for me," Mangiafuoco said.

"I'm supposed to go to school."

"School teaches you to sit still. I'll teach you to be seen."

Pinocchio went with the puppeteer.

This is the first lesson, and it's one he'll learn over and over, in different settings with different characters, before it finally sticks: the exciting option and the right option are almost never the same option, and the people who offer you excitement are almost never doing it for your benefit.

The story of Pinocchio is the story of every wrong turn.

He went with the puppeteer instead of school. He went with the Fox and the Cat instead of home. He went to the Land of Toys instead of keeping his promise. Every crossroads, every fork, every moment where the path split into should and want, Pinocchio chose want, and the choosing was so consistent and so predictable that it would be funny if it weren't also heartbreaking, because behind every wrong turn was Geppetto, waiting, worrying, growing older in his workshop, alone again.

The Fox and the Cat.

They found him on the road — two con artists, one clever and one not, working in partnership the way cons always work: the smart one plans, the dumb one provides muscle, and the mark provides everything else. They told Pinocchio about a field where you could plant gold coins and they'd grow into gold trees, which is a metaphor for every get-rich-quick scheme that has ever existed and will ever exist: give us your money and we'll make it more.

Pinocchio gave them his money. His money — the five gold coins Mangiafuoco had given him, a gift born of an unexpected moment of generosity from a man who was not, generally, generous. Pinocchio buried the coins in a field and waited for trees.

No trees.

The Fox and the Cat took the coins and left. Pinocchio was alone, in a field, at night, with nothing — no money, no school, no home, no plan — and the realization, slow and cold, that he'd been fooled.

The nose. You know about the nose.

Every time he lied, it grew. An inch, two inches, a foot — extending from his face like a branch, like an accusation, like the physical manifestation of every untruth given visible form. It was cruel and it was effective and it was the most obvious metaphor in the history of metaphors: lies take up space. They extend outward. They change the shape of the person telling them until the person is unrecognizable, until they're more lie than person, more nose than face.

Pinocchio lied constantly. He lied to Geppetto (I went to school), to the fairy (I haven't been with the Fox and Cat), to himself (I'll go home tomorrow). Each lie grew the nose, and each time, the fairy — who appeared periodically, less like a guardian angel and more like a parole officer — would shrink it back, and Pinocchio would promise to be good, and the promise would hold for about eleven minutes before the next shiny thing appeared.

The Land of Toys.

This is where the story gets dark, and where it means the most, and where Pinocchio — and every person who recognizes themselves in him — hits bottom.

A boy named Lampwick invited him. Lampwick was the kind of friend that parents warn you about and kids gravitate toward — reckless, loud, charismatic, allergic to rules. He told Pinocchio about a place where there was no school, no work, no bedtime, no obligations. A place where every day was play and every night was a party and the only rule was that there were no rules.

Pinocchio went. Of course he went. He'd been going his whole short life.

The Land of Toys was everything Lampwick promised. Candy and games and noise and the total, absolute absence of responsibility. For days — weeks? — Pinocchio played. He played with the feverish intensity of someone who suspects, on some level, that this can't be real, that there's a cost somewhere, that the bill is coming.

The bill came.

He woke one morning with furry ears. Then a tail. Then hooves. The transformation was gradual and then sudden, the way all consequences are — you ignore the warning signs until the warning signs become the thing itself. Pinocchio was turning into a donkey.

All the boys were. That was the business model — lure children to the Land of Toys, let them play until the play dissolved them into animals, and then sell the animals. The fun was the trap. The freedom was the cage. The lack of rules was the rule.

Pinocchio, half-donkey, terrified, escaped. He ran — on legs that were becoming hooves, from a place that was becoming his prison — and he threw himself into the sea.

The sea is where the story takes him to the place he needs to go.

The whale.

Inside the whale, in the dark, in the belly of something so enormous that it had its own weather and its own tides and its own particular smell of salt and digestion, Pinocchio found Geppetto.

The old man had been looking for him. For months — since Pinocchio had first run away, Geppetto had been searching. He'd built a boat. He'd sailed into the Mediterranean. He'd been swallowed by the whale, because the universe has a sense of dramatic irony, and because the story needed them to end up in the same impossible place.

Geppetto was thinner. Older. He'd been living inside the whale on raw fish and candlelight, surviving on the stubborn refusal to die before he found his son. His clothes were rags. His hands — the hands that had carved Pinocchio from pine, that had given him blue eyes and a too-long nose and life itself — his hands were shaking.

"Papa," Pinocchio said.

It was the first time he'd said it, and the word broke open inside him like a seed. Papa. Not Geppetto, not the old man, not the craftsman who made me. Papa. The word that means you are mine and I am yours and the distance between us is my fault and I'm sorry.

"Pinocchio," said Geppetto.

They held each other. In the belly of a whale, in the dark, in the place where the story had always been leading — not to the Land of Toys, not to the puppet theater, not to the field of gold — but here. To the moment where the boy stops running and the father stops chasing and they meet, finally, in the dark, and the meeting is enough.

They escaped. The story's mechanics involve fire and sneezing and a daring swim through open water, but the mechanics don't matter. What matters is that Pinocchio carried Geppetto. The old man couldn't swim — he was weak and cold and had been inside a whale — and Pinocchio, who was still partly wooden and partly donkey and entirely terrified, put his father on his back and swam.

He swam for hours. Through waves that tried to pull them under, through cold that tried to numb him, through the particular exhaustion that goes past the body and into the will. He swam because his father was on his back and drowning was not an option and for the first time in his short, chaotic, wrong-turn life, the right thing and the hard thing were the same thing, and he chose it.

They made it to shore.

Geppetto was unconscious. Pinocchio carried him to a cottage — someone's cottage, a stranger's kindness — and he put the old man in a bed and he sat beside him and he didn't leave.

He worked. For the first time. He drew water from the well, not because it was fun but because Geppetto needed water. He wove baskets, because a man in the village would pay for baskets, and the payment bought milk, which he gave to Geppetto. He did this every day — the repetitive, unglamorous, invisible work of caring for someone who can't care for themselves — and he did it without complaint, not because he'd become a saint but because he'd finally understood something that had been true all along:

Love is not a feeling. It's a series of actions. It's the basket and the milk and the sitting beside the bed and the not leaving. It's the opposite of every shiny thing that pulled him away. It's boring and it's daily and it's the only thing that's real.

He woke one morning and the wood was gone.

He didn't feel it happen. Like all real transformations, it happened in the space between waking and knowing — one moment he was pine, and the next he was skin. He looked at his hands. Flesh. Warm. The grain of the wood was gone, replaced by the lines of a palm, a human palm, with a lifeline and a heartline and the particular, unrepeatable pattern that means: you are the only one of you.

He looked in the mirror. A boy looked back. Not a puppet. Not a marionette. Not a wooden thing pretending to be real. A boy. A real boy, with real eyes — still blue, still Rosella's blue, some things survive the transformation — and a real nose, proportional, honest, belonging to a face that had earned the right to be called human.

Geppetto was awake. Geppetto was sitting up in bed, looking at Pinocchio, and his face was doing the thing that faces do when joy and grief and wonder and relief arrive simultaneously and there's not enough room for all of them: it was breaking and rebuilding at the same time, like a wave crashing and forming, over and over, in the space of a single breath.

"Papa," said Pinocchio.

"My boy," said Geppetto.

The old puppet — the wooden one, the original Pinocchio, with the strings and the painted eyes and the too-long nose — lay slumped in the corner of the room. Limp. Empty. The shell that had been shed, the way all shells are eventually shed by the things growing inside them.

Pinocchio looked at it. At himself. At the version of himself that had run from school, and lied to the fairy, and buried gold in a field, and turned half-donkey in a land of toys, and been swallowed by a whale. He looked at all of that, and he didn't feel shame — he felt something larger and more generous: recognition. That was me. That was where I started. And this is where I am.

He never became perfect. Let's be clear about that. The real boy still had the puppet's impulses — the wandering eye, the easy distraction, the pull toward the shiny thing. He still lied sometimes. His nose didn't grow, but his conscience did, and a growing conscience is worse than a growing nose because you can see a nose and laugh at it, but a conscience just sits inside you, heavy and warm and impossible to ignore.

He grew up. He learned the trade — Geppetto's trade, the one his father had been trying to teach him since the moment he was carved. He made puppets. Small ones, mostly — children's toys, marionettes with jointed limbs and painted faces and strings.

He always included the strings.

Not because the puppets needed them — they were just toys, just wood, they'd never dance on their own. But because the strings were a reminder. Of where he'd started. Of the moment he'd snapped them, standing on the workbench, declaring I can move on my own. Of how wrong he'd been — not about the moving, but about what moving means.

Moving on your own doesn't mean cutting the strings. It means choosing which strings to keep. The ones that connect you to the people who love you. The ones that pull you home when you've wandered too far. The ones that hold you up when your legs — wooden or otherwise — can't hold you themselves.

Geppetto lived to see his son become a man. A real man, which is different from a real boy the way a tree is different from a seed — larger, steadier, capable of providing shade. The workshop smelled like pine shavings and linseed oil and something else now — something warm, something alive, something that loneliness had vacated to make room for.

The house was not empty anymore.

Goodnight to Pinocchio, who became real the hard way — which is the only way.

Goodnight to Geppetto, who carved a boy from pine and love and loneliness, and got back more than he put in, which is the miracle of parenthood, which is the miracle of making anything.

Goodnight to the nose that grew, and the donkey ears that sprouted, and the whale that swallowed them both. Goodnight to every wrong turn, because wrong turns are how you find the right road, eventually, tired and half-wooden and smelling like whale.

And goodnight to you. If you're still part puppet. If the strings are still attached, or recently cut, or being chosen one by one. If the nose grows when you lie and the conscience aches when you don't and the road between here and real is longer than you thought it would be.

Keep swimming. Your Papa is on your back, or waiting at home, or somewhere in the belly of something enormous, and the shore is there, and the morning is coming, and the wood — the beautiful, faithful, patient wood that carried you this far — the wood is almost gone.

And what's underneath it is you.

Goodnight.

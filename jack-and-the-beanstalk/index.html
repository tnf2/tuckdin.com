Jack and the Beanstalk

Originally by English fairy tale — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 10 min read 📖 2,243 words 🌙 Best read aloud

English fairy tale — Retold for grown-ups

Jack's mother told him to sell the cow, and he came home with beans.

Let's sit with that for a moment, because the story always rushes past it — the beans are magic, the beanstalk grows, adventure ensues — but before any of that, there's a woman standing in a kitchen, looking at her son, who has just traded the last valuable thing they own for a handful of beans because a stranger on the road told him they were special.

Her name was Margaret. No one in the story ever asks.

She'd been running the farm alone since Jack's father died, which was six years ago, or seven — long enough that the specific number had blurred but the weight of it hadn't. She'd kept the animals fed and the roof patched and the creditors at bay through a combination of stubbornness, cleverness, and the particular kind of endurance that single mothers develop the way calluses develop: not because they want to, but because the alternative is being rubbed raw.

The cow — Milky White, which was a name Jack had given her when he was five and Margaret hadn't had the heart to override — was the last of the livestock. She'd stopped giving milk a month ago, and a cow that doesn't give milk is a mouth to feed with nothing coming in, which is a math problem Margaret didn't need anyone to explain.

"Sell her at market," she told Jack. "Get the best price you can. We need it for seed, and flour, and the tax that's due next month."

Jack was eighteen. Old enough to go to market. Old enough to be trusted.

He came home with beans.

Margaret looked at the beans. She looked at Jack. She opened her mouth, and what came out was not what she wanted to say — she wanted to say how could you, she wanted to say we needed that money, she wanted to say your father would have — but none of those things came out. What came out was silence, because sometimes the disappointment is so large that language can't carry it.

She threw the beans out the window. Not ceremonially. Not in a fit of rage. She threw them the way you throw away junk mail — with the flat, tired contempt of someone who doesn't have the energy for drama.

Jack went to bed hungry. Margaret went to bed hungry. The beans lay in the dirt outside the window, and the night was long, and neither of them slept well.

The beanstalk was there in the morning.

Not a plant. A structure. It rose from the patch of dirt beneath the window like a green cathedral, thick as an oak at the base, twisting upward through the morning mist and into the clouds and beyond, so high that the top was not visible, was perhaps not even there, was perhaps still growing, reaching for something that only beanstalks and dreamers and eighteen-year-olds understand.

Margaret stood at the window and stared.

Jack stood beside her and said, "See?"

"I see a plant," she said. "I don't see flour or seed money or the tax payment."

But she was staring at it the way people stare at things that contradict everything they know — not with wonder, not yet, but with the unsettled, trembling feeling that comes right before wonder, the feeling of a worldview cracking.

"I'm going to climb it," Jack said.

"No."

"Mum—"

"Absolutely not. You traded our cow for beans that grew into — whatever this is — and now you want to climb it into the sky. No. We're going to the market, we're going to see if anyone will buy beanstalk wood, and we're going to figure out how to eat this week."

Jack climbed it anyway.

He went while she was in the henhouse, checking for eggs that hadn't been there yesterday and wouldn't be there today. He grabbed the lowest branch — thick and green and ridged, warm under his hands — and he pulled himself up, and then up again, and then again, and the ground fell away beneath him with a speed that should have been terrifying but wasn't, because Jack was eighteen, and eighteen-year-olds believe in their own invincibility the way fish believe in water: completely, unconsciously, and incorrectly.

He climbed for a long time. The air thinned. The clouds thickened. The world below became a quilt of green and brown and the tiny, impossible shape of his mother's house, and then the clouds closed around him like cotton and he couldn't see anything at all, and then —

He was above them.

The land above the clouds was not what he expected.

He expected heaven, maybe, or some kind of paradise — golden fields, singing birds, the kind of place you see in paintings where everyone looks clean and well-rested. What he got was a road. A long, grey, ordinary road stretching across a flat, grey, ordinary landscape beneath a sky that was white and featureless and close. The air smelled like iron.

At the end of the road: a castle.

Not the fairy tale kind. Not turrets and banners and a moat with swans. This was the other kind — the real kind, the kind built for keeping things in and keeping things out. Massive stone walls. A door you could drive a cart through. The kind of building that says whoever lives here has a lot of things and does not intend to share them.

Jack went inside because Jack went inside things. This was both his greatest quality and his greatest flaw, and they were the same quality, and he would spend the rest of his life trying to figure out when courage becomes recklessness and whether the line between them is real or just something cautious people invented to make themselves feel better about staying home.

The castle belonged to a giant.

Not a metaphorical giant. Not a large man. A giant — a being of such impossible scale that the furniture was the size of rooms and the rooms were the size of fields and the smell that pervaded everything was the heavy, animal, overwhelming smell of something enormous and alive, like standing inside a heartbeat.

The giant's wife found Jack first. She was large — everything here was large — but her eyes were regular-sized in their kindness, and she hid him in the oven, which was cold and dark and smelled like ash, and she said, "He'll eat you if he finds you. He eats everything."

"FEE FI FO FUM," came the voice, from somewhere deep in the castle, and the floor shook, and the walls shook, and Jack's teeth shook in his skull, and he understood — for the first time, really understood — that he was very far from home and the ground was very far below and his mother had been right.

"I SMELL THE BLOOD OF AN ENGLISHMAN."

The wife calmed the giant. Fed him. Eventually, he slept — the heavy, crushing sleep of enormous things, a sleep that shook dust from the rafters with every exhale.

Jack crept out.

On the table, beside the sleeping giant: a hen. Not a regular hen. A hen that, as Jack watched, shifted on her perch and produced — with a small, apologetic cluck — a golden egg.

Jack took the hen.

He took her and he ran — down the road, through the clouds, down the beanstalk, hand over hand, the hen tucked under one arm making small, distressed sounds, and behind him, far above, the giant woke and roared and the beanstalk trembled with the weight of something enormous and furious descending.

"AXE!" Jack shouted, and Margaret, who had been standing at the base of the beanstalk for hours, sick with worry, furious with fear, holding an axe because she'd known — somehow, the way mothers know — that an axe would be needed — she swung.

She swung hard. She swung with twenty years of raising a son alone and six years of grief and a month of no milk and a night of beans and the particular strength that comes from being absolutely, completely, terrifyingly done.

The beanstalk fell.

The sound it made was not like a tree falling. It was like a bridge collapsing, like an idea breaking, like the connection between here and there being severed permanently and completely. It crashed across the fields and the clouds above closed like a wound healing and the giant — wherever he was on that stalk — fell with it, and the earth shook once, hard, and then was still.

They kept the hen.

Of course they kept the hen. The hen laid golden eggs, one a day, and they sold them — not all at once, not greedily, but steadily, the way Margaret had done everything for the past six years: one day at a time, one problem at a time, one egg at a time.

The farm recovered. Then it thrived. Then it became the kind of place that people pointed to and said they're doing well, aren't they? without knowing the full story, because full stories are messy and people prefer the clean version.

Jack grew up. He married a woman who was smarter than him, which he had the rare good sense to recognize and appreciate. He ran the farm. He raised children who he told, with great seriousness, never to trade cows for beans — and then he'd catch his mother's eye across the room and she'd shake her head, and they'd both smile, because the truth was more complicated than the lesson.

The truth was that the beans had worked.

The stupid, reckless, irresponsible, indefensible decision to trade a cow for a handful of beans from a stranger on the road had, against all odds and logic, worked. And Margaret knew this, and it infuriated her, because it meant the world was not as orderly as she needed it to be. It meant that sometimes the careful people are wrong and the reckless people are right and there's no system for telling which is which in advance.

But here's the part the story doesn't tell you:

Jack went back to the stump sometimes. The place where the beanstalk had been, now just a wide, rough circle of dead wood, slowly rotting into the earth. He'd sit on it in the evenings, after the farm was quiet and the children were asleep and the golden egg had been collected and the day was done.

He'd look up.

The clouds were just clouds. There was no road, no castle, no giant, no land above. The connection was severed. The beanstalk was gone. Whatever was up there — whoever was up there — was unreachable now, and had been since his mother swung the axe.

He thought about the giant's wife. The one who'd hidden him. The one who'd risked her safety for a stranger small enough to fit in her oven. He wondered what happened to her after the giant fell. He wondered if she was still up there, in that enormous castle, alone now, with nothing but rooms the size of fields and a silence the size of the sky.

He never found out. Some doors, once closed, stay closed. Some beanstalks, once cut, don't grow back. Some debts can't be repaid because the person you owe them to is on the other side of the clouds, and the clouds have sealed shut, and all you have is a stump and a memory and the uncomfortable knowledge that your adventure was someone else's catastrophe.

That's the thing about Jack and the Beanstalk that no one talks about. Jack is the hero. But Jack is also the thief. He climbed into someone's home, took their things, and cut down the only bridge between their worlds. The giant was terrible, yes. But the wife was kind. And Jack took from them both equally.

Growing up is learning to hold both of those things — the adventure and the cost, the golden egg and the severed stalk, the triumph and the guilt — and not needing one to cancel out the other.

Jack held them. He sat on the stump and he held them, and the evening turned to night, and the stars came out — the same stars, above and below the clouds — and eventually he went inside, where his mother was reading by the fire and his wife was putting the children to bed, and the hen was on her perch, and the egg would come in the morning, one more, always one more, steady as a heartbeat.

And that was enough.

Goodnight to Jack, who climbed because he couldn't not. Goodnight to Margaret, who swung the axe because someone had to. Goodnight to the hen and her impossible eggs and the quiet miracle of enough.

Goodnight to the beanstalk that isn't there anymore. Goodnight to the land above the clouds that might have been a dream but left golden proof that it wasn't.

And goodnight to every reckless, stupid, beautiful decision you've ever made that worked out despite itself. You got lucky. You know you got lucky. Keep the egg. Remember the cost. Sit with both.

Goodnight.

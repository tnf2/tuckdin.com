Jack and the Beanstalk

Originally by English fairy tale — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 10 min read 📖 807 words 🌙 Best read aloud

English fairy tale — Retold for grown-ups

Jack's mother told him to sell the cow, and he came home with beans.

Let's sit with that. Before the beanstalk and the giant and the gold — there's a woman in a kitchen, looking at her son, who just traded their last valuable thing for a handful of beans because a stranger said they were special.

Her name was Margaret. Nobody in the story ever asks.

She'd been running the farm alone since Jack's father died. Six years of keeping animals fed, the roof patched, creditors at bay — stubbornness, cleverness, and the endurance single mothers develop like calluses.

The cow — Milky White, named by Jack when he was five — stopped giving milk a month ago. A cow that doesn't give milk is a mouth to feed with nothing coming in.

"Sell her. Get the best price. We need it for seed and flour and the tax that's due."

Jack was eighteen. Old enough to be trusted.

Beans.

Margaret looked at them. Opened her mouth. What came out was silence, because sometimes the disappointment is too large for language.

She threw them out the window — not in rage, just with the tired contempt you give junk mail.

They went to bed hungry.

The beanstalk was there in the morning. Not a plant — a structure. Thick as an oak at the base, twisting upward through the mist into the clouds and beyond.

"See?" said Jack.

"I see a plant. I don't see flour or the tax payment."

"I'm climbing it."

"Absolutely not."

He climbed it while she was checking for eggs that weren't there.

Hand over hand, ground falling away. He was eighteen. Eighteen-year-olds believe in their own invincibility the way fish believe in water — completely, unconsciously, incorrectly.

Above the clouds: a road. Long, grey, flat. Iron smell in the air. At the end — a castle. Not the fairy tale kind. The keeping things in kind.

Inside: a giant. Not metaphorical. A being so large the furniture was room-sized and the smell was overwhelming — like standing inside a heartbeat.

The giant's wife hid Jack in the cold oven. "He'll eat you if he finds you."

"FEE FI FO FUM." The floor shook. The walls shook. Jack's teeth shook.

The wife calmed him. Fed him. He slept.

Jack crept out. On the table: a hen that laid a golden egg as he watched.

He took the hen and ran. Down the beanstalk, hen under one arm making distressed sounds.

"AXE!" he shouted.

Margaret was at the base. She'd been there for hours, holding an axe, because she'd known — the way mothers know — that an axe would be needed.

She swung. Hard. Twenty years of raising a son alone and six years of grief and a night of beans. The beanstalk fell like a bridge collapsing.

They kept the hen. One golden egg a day, sold steadily — the way Margaret did everything. One problem at a time.

The farm recovered. Then thrived. Jack married someone smarter than him, which he had the sense to appreciate.

The beans had worked. The stupid, reckless decision had worked. Margaret knew this, and it infuriated her, because it meant the world wasn't as orderly as she needed it to be.

But Jack went back to the stump sometimes. Where the beanstalk had been — just dead wood, rotting into the earth. He'd sit there in the evenings and look up.

The clouds were just clouds. The connection was severed.

He thought about the giant's wife. The one who hid him. Who risked her safety for a stranger small enough to fit in her oven. He wondered what happened to her.

He never found out. Some doors, once closed, stay closed. Some debts can't be repaid because the person you owe is on the other side of the clouds.

Jack is the hero. Jack is also the thief. He climbed into someone's home, took their things, and cut down the only bridge. The giant was terrible. The wife was kind. He took from both.

Growing up is holding the adventure and the cost at the same time, without needing one to cancel the other.

He sat on the stump, and the stars came out, and eventually he went inside — where his mother was reading, his wife putting the kids to bed, and the hen on her perch.

The egg would come in the morning. One more. Always one more.

Enough.

Goodnight to Jack, who climbed because he couldn't not. Goodnight to Margaret, who swung the axe because someone had to.

Goodnight to every reckless decision that worked out despite itself. You got lucky. Keep the egg. Remember the cost.

Goodnight.

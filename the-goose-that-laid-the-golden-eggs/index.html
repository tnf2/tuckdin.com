The Goose That Laid the Golden Eggs

Originally by Aesop — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 7 min read 📖 1,957 words 🌙 Best read aloud

The farmer found the egg on a Tuesday.

He almost missed it. He was doing the morning rounds — the same rounds he'd done every morning for twenty-two years, the same circuit past the henhouse and the goat pen and the barn where the tools lived in a state of organized neglect. He was not paying attention. Morning rounds are not the kind of thing you pay attention to. They are the kind of thing your body does while your mind is somewhere else — thinking about the fence that needed fixing, or the price of grain, or whether the ache in his left knee was the kind that went away or the kind that stayed.

The egg was in the straw beneath the grey goose.

He almost stepped on it. But something caught his eye — a glint, a warmth, a color that didn't belong in a henhouse the way a symphony doesn't belong in a parking lot. He stopped. He looked down. And there it was.

Gold.

Not golden. Not yellowish. Not reminiscent of gold. Gold. Actual, dense, warm-to-the-touch, impossibly heavy gold, shaped exactly like an egg, sitting in the straw beneath a perfectly ordinary grey goose who looked at him with the blank, unreadable expression that geese have perfected over millennia of being geese.

He picked it up. It was heavy — heavier than it had any right to be. The weight of it settled into his palm the way significance settles into a moment, slowly and then all at once. He turned it over. It was smooth. It was flawless. It was, by any reasonable measure, impossible.

"Huh," said the farmer.

He was not a dramatic man. He was the kind of man who, upon discovering that the laws of nature had been suspended in his henhouse, said huh and then stood there for a while, holding the egg, trying to decide if he was dreaming or losing his mind or both.

He took it to the kitchen. His wife was making coffee. She looked at the egg. She looked at him. She picked up the egg, felt its weight, held it to the window light.

"Huh," she said.

They were well-matched.

He took it to town. The goldsmith weighed it, tested it, scraped a tiny line across the bottom with a needle, and looked up at the farmer with an expression that the farmer had never seen on a goldsmith before and would describe later as confused by his own happiness.

"It's gold," said the goldsmith.

"I know," said the farmer, who hadn't known but wasn't going to admit that now.

"Where did you get this?"

"My goose."

The goldsmith stared at him. The farmer stared back. The goldsmith decided that whatever was happening, he wanted to be on the right side of it, and he paid the farmer handsomely. Very handsomely. Enough for the fence that needed fixing and a new roof and the kind of boots that don't let in water and a dress for his wife that she'd been looking at in the catalog for three months without mentioning, because she was the kind of woman who looked at things without mentioning them.

That night, they sat at the kitchen table and looked at the money and felt something they hadn't felt in a long time: the absence of worry. Not happiness, exactly — that would come later — but the removal of the weight they'd been carrying so long they'd forgotten it was there. Like taking off a heavy coat you'd been wearing indoors. You didn't know how heavy it was until it was off.

The next morning, another egg.

Same goose. Same straw. Same gold. Same weight, same warmth, same impossibility, now slightly less impossible because it had happened before.

And the morning after that. And the one after that.

Every day, one golden egg. Every day, a small fortune in the straw. The farmer collected them with a calm that was either admirable or insane — he brought them inside, he took them to the goldsmith, he deposited the money, he went home. He didn't tell anyone. His wife didn't tell anyone. They had lived quietly for twenty-two years and they saw no reason to stop now.

But money has a way of being noticed.

The fence got fixed. Then replaced entirely. The roof went from patched to new. A second barn appeared, and then a third. The boots became regular purchases rather than events. The catalog dresses became a wardrobe. None of it was extravagant — the farmer didn't have the gene for extravagance — but it was steady, and in a small village, steady improvement is louder than any single purchase.

People talked. People always talk. At the market, at the well, over fences. Have you seen their new barn? And the stonework on the house? Where's the money coming from? The theories ranged from sensible (inheritance) to creative (buried treasure) to scandalous (something involving the widow three towns over, which the farmer's wife found so absurd she actually laughed, which she almost never did, which made the farmer fall a little more in love with her, which is the kind of thing that happens when you've been married twenty-two years and still surprise each other).

The farmer didn't mind the talk. What he minded — what started as a small thing and grew — was the waiting.

Every morning, he'd walk to the henhouse. Every morning, the egg was there. But the walk got longer. Not physically — it was the same thirty steps it had always been — but inside his chest, the walk stretched. Because what if it wasn't there? What if this was the morning the goose decided to be ordinary again? What if the impossible thing that had happened every day for months simply... stopped?

This is what money does. Not the having of it — the expecting of it. Once you've built your life around a miracle, the miracle becomes a requirement, and requirements are the opposite of gifts. A gift makes you grateful. A requirement makes you anxious.

The farmer started waking earlier. Three in the morning, then two. He'd lie in bed and think about the egg and the goose and the gold, and the thinking was not pleasant. It was the particular, grinding anxiety of a man who has been given everything he wanted and now lives in terror of it being taken away.

His wife noticed. Of course she noticed. She noticed the way he looked at the goose — not with affection, not with gratitude, but with a kind of hunger that had nothing to do with food.

"You're different," she said, one evening.

"I'm fine," he said.

"You're not fine. You're not sleeping. You're not eating properly. You spend more time staring at that goose than you spend talking to me."

"That goose is the reason we have any of this."

"We had a life before the goose."

He didn't answer. Because she was right, and the truth of it was uncomfortable. They had a life before the goose. It was a smaller life, a harder life, a life of patched roofs and leaking boots. But it had been theirs, fully and without anxiety, and he hadn't spent any part of it lying awake at two in the morning, afraid.

The idea came to him the way bad ideas always come: quietly, reasonably, dressed up in logic.

If the goose laid one golden egg a day, then the gold was inside the goose. All of it. Every egg that would ever be laid — a lifetime of gold, sitting right there, inside one grey bird. Why wait? Why endure the daily anxiety, the morning walk, the terrible suspense of will it be there? Why take the gold one piece at a time when you could have it all at once?

He didn't share the idea with his wife. He knew, somewhere beneath the logic, that the idea would not survive her scrutiny. Bad ideas rarely do. That's why they prefer to be alone.

He went to the henhouse on a Wednesday morning, early, before dawn. The goose was sleeping. He stood over her for a long time, and in the grey pre-dawn light, she looked like exactly what she was: a goose. An ordinary, grey, sleeping goose. Nothing about her suggested gold or miracles or the impossible. She was breathing softly. Her feathers rose and fell.

He did it quickly.

And inside — inside there was nothing. No gold. No reservoir, no hidden chamber, no vault of future eggs. Just... a goose. Blood and bone and feather and the warm, ordinary machinery of a living thing that was no longer living.

He stood in the henhouse, holding what he'd done, and the silence was the loudest thing he'd ever heard.

His wife found him there an hour later. She looked at the goose. She looked at him. She didn't say anything for a long time.

She didn't need to.

He knew. He'd known before he did it, really, in the part of himself that was still the man who said huh and took things one day at a time and didn't need to understand miracles in order to receive them. That man had tried to stop him. That man had been overruled by the other one — the new one, the one the gold had built, the one who couldn't sleep and couldn't wait and couldn't accept that some things come to you slowly, one day at a time, and that the slowness is not a flaw in the system but the whole point of it.

"Why?" his wife said, finally.

"I wanted it all at once," he said. And his voice broke on the word all, because he heard, as he said it, how small it sounded. How greedy. How stupid. All at once. As if the daily miracle — the egg in the straw, the walk to the goldsmith, the slow, steady accumulation of a life getting better — as if all of that was somehow not enough.

Enough. There's that word again.

They went on. People do. The money they'd saved lasted a while, and then it didn't, and then they were back to the life they'd had before — the patched roof, the leaking boots, the catalog looked at but not ordered from. It was harder this time, not because the life was worse, but because they knew what they'd lost.

Or rather — what he'd destroyed.

His wife forgave him, eventually, because that's what people who love each other do with the unforgivable things. She forgave him not all at once but slowly, one day at a time, which was — he realized, too late — exactly how the gold had come. Exactly how everything good comes.

One day at a time.

The henhouse still stood. He cleaned it out, kept other birds. Regular birds, who laid regular eggs, and he collected them every morning with a gratitude that would have seemed insane to the man he'd been a year ago. A regular egg. A regular morning. A regular life.

It was enough.

It had always been enough.

Goodnight. And if there's something in your life that's giving you one golden egg a day — a person, a job, a place, a quiet happiness that shows up reliably and without drama —

Don't cut it open.

Don't try to get it all at once.

Just say thank you. Collect the egg. Go home.

Tomorrow there'll be another one.

Goodnight.

The Frog Prince

Originally by Brothers Grimm — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 8 min read 📖 757 words 🌙 Best read aloud

The princess dropped the ball because she wasn't paying attention. Golden ball, heavy, a gift from her father. It slipped and hit the well with a sound like a swallowed word.

Ploonk. Gone.

The well was deep. Real deep — count to three before the splash.

She sat on the edge and cried. Not delicately. The ugly kind — gasping, swollen, every pretense stripped away.

"That's a lot of noise for a ball," said a voice. She looked down. A frog, half in shadow on the mossy rim.

Not a handsome frog. There's no such thing. Green, wet, eyes bulging. Sitting with a patience that could be cold-blooded or just cold.

"Can you get it?"

"I can. But I'd like something in return. Not jewels. I want to eat at your table. Sleep on your pillow. Be treated like a companion, not a curiosity."

"Fine. Yes. Whatever. Just get my ball."

She said it the way people say things when they're desperate — fast, without thinking, planning to forget the promise the moment the crisis passed.

The frog dove. Long time. Came back with the ball in his mouth, spat it on the grass.

She grabbed it. She ran home.

He came that evening. Wet, rhythmic slapping on the stone steps.

Knock knock knock.

"Princess. You made a promise."

Her father looked up from dinner. She told him — the ball, the well, the deal.

"You made a promise," he said.

"To a frog."

"You made a promise. Open the door."

The frog hopped in. Damp prints on the floor. The servants stared. He sat at the table and ate with quiet precision — the carefulness of someone who knows he's unwelcome and refuses to give extra reasons.

After dinner: "The pillow."

"No."

"You promised."

Her father's look said: you know what to do, and I'm going to sit here being disappointed until you do it.

She carried him upstairs at arm's length. Set him on her pillow.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't."

"I know this isn't what you wanted."

"You don't know anything about what I want."

"I know you want the ball more than the promise. Most people do. They want the thing more than the cost of the thing."

Nobody had ever spoken to her like this. Not her father, not her tutors, not the boys at court. Nobody had looked at her and said, plainly: this is who you are.

"Who are you?" she said.

"Right now? A frog."

"Before?"

He blinked. That slow, whole-face frog blink.

"Someone who broke a promise. A big one. And this is what that looks like."

She didn't sleep. She lay in the dark listening to him breathe — small, wet breaths. She thought about promises. The distance between making them and keeping them. How easy yes, anything is when you're desperate. How the desperation fades and the promise doesn't.

She thought about the frog. He hadn't been rude or demanding. He'd asked for a meal, a pillow, company. Not love. Just don't forget me the moment you get what you want.

Morning. He was awake, watching dawn come through the window.

"It's nice, this," he said. "The morning. I forget sometimes."

Something shifted in her. Not a thunderbolt. A door opening an inch. She saw him — not the wet skin or the bulging eyes. Him. A person watching the sunrise, grateful for the morning.

She reached out and touched him. Gently. On the top of his head. A gesture that said not I love you but I see you.

The change was slow. The way real changes are. Green fading. Skin warming. Shape lengthening. Over a quiet minute — a man. Sitting on her pillow, blinking in the light, looking at his hands like someone returned something they thought they'd lost forever.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what?"

"For keeping the promise. Even when you didn't want to. Especially then."

The golden ball sat on the nightstand, catching morning light.

It was just a ball. Always was.

The promise was the real gold.

Goodnight to the princess, who learned that keeping your word costs more than breaking it, and is worth more.

Goodnight to the frog, who waited by the well for someone willing to see past the worst of him.

And goodnight to every promise you've made that you didn't want to keep. The hard ones count the most.

Goodnight.

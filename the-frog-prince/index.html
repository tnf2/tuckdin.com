The Frog Prince

Originally by Brothers Grimm — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 8 min read 📖 2,075 words 🌙 Best read aloud

The princess dropped the ball because she wasn't paying attention.

That's not the version you heard as a child. The version you heard probably made it sound like an accident — a fumble, a slip, an innocent mistake by a golden well on a summer afternoon. But the truth is less forgiving and more human: she wasn't paying attention because she was thinking about something else, and the ball — a golden ball, heavy and smooth and given to her by her father for her sixteenth birthday — slipped from her fingers and dropped into the well with a sound like a swallowed word.

Ploonk.

Gone.

The well was deep. Not the decorative kind with a bucket and a crank and ivy growing up the stones — a real well, ancient, the water at the bottom so far down that dropping a stone produced a count of three before the splash. The ball had fallen into something that didn't give things back.

The princess sat on the edge and cried.

Not delicately. Not the way princesses are supposed to cry — single tear, trembling lip, photogenic grief. She cried the way people actually cry when something they love is gone and they can't fix it: ugly, wet, gasping, the kind of crying that makes your face swell and your nose run and strips away every pretense of composure until you're just a person, sitting by a hole in the ground, wanting something back.

"That's a lot of noise for a ball," said a voice.

She looked around. No one.

"Down here."

She looked down. On the mossy stone rim of the well, half in shadow, sat a frog.

He was not a handsome frog. There's no such thing as a handsome frog, despite what certain fairy tales would have you believe. He was green and wet and his eyes bulged and his skin had that particular moist texture that makes people who are not fond of amphibians take a step back. He sat very still, the way frogs do, with a patience that comes from being cold-blooded — literally, not metaphorically, though perhaps metaphorically too.

"I dropped my ball," the princess said, because she didn't know what else to say to a talking frog, and stating the obvious felt like a reasonable start.

"I know. I watched."

"Can you get it?"

The frog blinked. Frogs blink slowly, with their whole face. It gives every blink the weight of a considered opinion.

"I can," he said. "But I'd like something in return."

The princess wiped her eyes. "What do you want? Jewels? Gold? I have a necklace—"

"I don't want your necklace. I want to eat at your table. I want to sleep on your pillow. I want to be treated like a companion, not a curiosity. I want you to keep your promise once you make it."

The princess looked at the frog. The frog looked at the princess. A negotiation was happening, the kind that happens between two people who want different things and have exactly one point of leverage each.

"Fine," she said. "Yes. Whatever you want. Just please get my ball."

She said it the way people say things when they're desperate — quickly, without thinking, with the full intention of forgetting the promise the moment the crisis passes. The ball was the only real thing. The promise was just words, and words are cheap, especially when you're sixteen and crying and something you love is at the bottom of a well.

The frog dove.

He was gone for a long time. Long enough that the princess began to wonder if he'd drowned, and then felt guilty for wondering, and then felt guilty for the guilt, because she wasn't sure if feeling bad about a frog drowning counted as genuine sadness or just good manners.

He came back with the ball in his mouth. He spat it onto the grass at her feet — golden, wet, perfect.

She grabbed it. She held it to her chest. She turned and ran.

She ran home to the castle and up the stairs and into her room and shut the door and did not think about the frog. She put him in the same place you put things you've agreed to but don't want to do: the back of the mind, behind the curtain, where obligations go to be forgotten.

He came that evening.

She heard him before she saw him — a wet, rhythmic slapping on the stone steps of the castle, like a small, determined applause. Slap. Slap. Slap. Up the stairs, down the hall, to the dining room door.

Knock knock knock.

"Princess," said the voice, muffled by oak. "You made a promise."

The king — her father — looked up from his dinner. "Who's that?"

And because lying to your father at dinner is harder than lying to a frog by a well, the princess told him. The ball, the well, the promise. She told it quickly, the way you rip off a bandage, hoping the speed would minimize the damage.

The king listened. He set down his fork. He had the expression of a man who has just been handed a problem he didn't ask for but recognizes as important.

"You made a promise," he said.

"To a frog."

"You made a promise."

"He's a frog, Father."

"And you're a princess. Open the door."

She opened the door.

The frog hopped in. He was wet and he was small and he left damp prints on the floor and the servants stared and the princess's younger sister made a face that would, in a more modern era, have gone viral. He hopped to the table. He looked up at the princess.

"A plate?" he said.

She gave him a plate. She put food on it. He ate with a quiet, methodical precision that was somehow worse than if he'd been messy — the carefulness of someone who knows he's unwelcome and is determined to give no additional reasons.

The princess ate nothing. She sat with her arms crossed and her jaw set and the particular vibrating resentment of a person who is keeping a promise they never intended to keep and blaming the other party for holding them to it.

After dinner, the frog said, "The pillow."

"No."

"You promised."

She looked at her father. Her father looked at her. The look said: you know what you need to do, and I'm not going to make you do it, but I'm going to sit here and be disappointed until you do, which is worse.

She carried the frog upstairs. She held him at arm's length, the way you carry something you find distasteful but can't put down — a bag of trash, a difficult truth, a consequence. She set him on the pillow. Her pillow. The one she'd slept on every night since she was small, that smelled like her hair and her sleep and her most private, unguarded self.

The frog sat on it. He didn't say anything. He just sat there, small and green and wet on the white linen, and he watched her with those bulging, unblinking eyes.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't."

"I know this isn't what you wanted."

"You don't know anything about what I want."

"I know you want the ball more than the promise. Most people do. They want the thing more than the cost of the thing. That's not a criticism. It's just how people work."

The princess stared at him. No one had ever spoken to her like this — not her father, not her tutors, not the boys who came to court with their pressed shirts and their prepared compliments. No one had ever looked at her and said, plainly and without cruelty, this is who you are.

"Who are you?" she said.

"Right now? A frog."

"And before?"

He blinked. That slow, whole-face blink.

"Someone who made a mistake," he said. "A long time ago. Someone who broke a promise, actually. A big one, to someone who didn't deserve it. And this is what that looks like."

She didn't sleep that night. She lay in the dark and listened to the frog breathe — small, wet breaths, barely audible, the respiration of something that takes up almost no space in the world. She thought about promises. About the distance between making them and keeping them. About how easy it is to say yes, anything, whatever you want when you're desperate, and how the desperation fades and the promise doesn't.

She thought about the frog. About the fact that he hadn't been rude or demanding or unreasonable. He'd asked for exactly what he'd asked for — a meal, a pillow, company. Not love. Not affection. Just presence. Just don't forget me the moment you get what you want.

In the morning, she woke to find the frog on the edge of the pillow, awake, watching the dawn come through the window. The light was gold — that early, tentative gold that the sun tries out before committing to the full performance of the day.

"It's nice, this," the frog said. "The morning. I forget, sometimes, that the morning is nice."

And something happened in the princess. Not a thunderbolt. Not a revelation. Just a small, quiet shift — a door opening an inch in a room that had been locked. She looked at the frog and she saw him. Not the wet skin or the bulging eyes or the inconvenience of him. Him. A person — whatever he was, a frog, a man, a consequence — sitting on her pillow, watching the sunrise, grateful for the morning.

She reached out and touched him. Not a kiss — the story you heard had a kiss, but the story you heard was simplified for children, and children need clear mechanisms, cause and effect, lip to skin and poof, magic. What actually happened was smaller and harder and more real: she touched him. Gently. On the top of his head, where the skin was cool and smooth. A gesture that said, not I love you, but I see you. You're here. I won't pretend you're not.

The change was not instant. It was slow, the way real changes are — a softening, a shifting, a gradual unfolding, like watching a time-lapse of something growing. The green faded. The skin warmed. The shape lengthened and filled and became, over the course of a long, quiet minute, a man.

He was not a prince. Or rather, he was, technically — there was a kingdom somewhere, and a throne he'd left empty, and a story he'd tell her later — but that wasn't the point. The point was that he was sitting on her pillow, blinking in the morning light, looking at his hands like someone who's just been returned something they thought they'd lost forever.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what?"

"For keeping the promise. Even when you didn't want to. Especially when you didn't want to."

They sat together for a while, in the morning light, not saying much. There would be time for the rest — the explanations, the kingdom, the future that unrolled ahead of them like a road neither of them had planned to walk. But for now, there was just this: two people in a room, one of whom had been something else an hour ago, both of whom were different than they'd been the day before.

The golden ball sat on the nightstand. It caught the light and threw small, shivering reflections across the ceiling.

It was just a ball. It had always been just a ball.

The promise was the real gold.

Goodnight to the princess, who learned that keeping your word costs more than breaking it, and is worth more too.

Goodnight to the frog, who waited by the well for someone willing to see past the worst of him.

Goodnight to every promise you've made that you didn't want to keep. The hard ones count the most.

And goodnight to the golden ball, at the bottom of whatever well you dropped it in. Maybe it's still there. Maybe it doesn't matter.

Some things you lose turn out to be less important than what you find on the way to getting them back.

Goodnight.

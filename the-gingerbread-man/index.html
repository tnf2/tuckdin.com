The Gingerbread Man

Originally by English fairy tale — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 8 min read 📖 2,106 words 🌙 Best read aloud

English fairy tale — Retold for grown-ups

The old woman made him on a Thursday.

Not for any particular reason. There was no grandchild coming, no holiday, no occasion that demanded gingerbread. She made him because the house was quiet and the afternoon was long and her hands wanted something to do, and when your hands want something to do, you let them. Idle hands are not the devil's workshop. Idle hands are just sad.

She mixed the dough the way she'd been mixing dough for forty years — by feel, not by recipe. Flour, molasses, ginger, cinnamon, a pinch of clove that she added with the kind of recklessness that only comes from decades of knowing exactly how reckless you can be. The dough came together under her palms, dark and fragrant and warm, and she rolled it flat on the floured board and cut it into the shape of a man.

A small man. Five inches tall, maybe six. She gave him raisin eyes and a frosting smile and three buttons down his front, because gingerbread men have always had three buttons, and she wasn't about to be the one to break tradition.

She put him in the oven and sat down to wait.

The kitchen smelled the way kitchens are supposed to smell — like someone is paying attention. Like someone cares enough to stand in a warm room and turn sugar and spice into something that didn't exist an hour ago. The old woman closed her eyes. The clock ticked. The oven hummed.

And then the oven door opened.

Not slowly, the way oven doors open when hinges loosen. Suddenly. With purpose. The door swung wide and out came the gingerbread man, sliding off the baking sheet and landing on the kitchen floor with a soft, warm thup, and then — impossibly, absurdly, with a confidence that had no business existing in something made of flour and molasses — he ran.

He ran across the kitchen tiles and out the door the old woman had left open because the afternoon was warm.

"Stop!" she said, which is what you say when something impossible happens and you haven't had time to think of anything better.

The gingerbread man didn't stop. He ran down the porch steps and across the yard and into the lane, and over his shoulder — he had shoulders now, apparently — he called back:

"Run, run, as fast as you can. You can't catch me — I'm the Gingerbread Man!"

His voice was high and bright and sweet, the way you'd expect a voice made of sugar and spice to sound. And there was something in it — not malice, not cruelty, but delight. The pure, uncomplicated delight of a thing that has just discovered it exists and can move and is fast and is free.

The old woman stood on her porch and watched him go. She did not chase him. She was seventy-three years old and her knees were not what they used to be and she had learned, over a long life, that chasing things that want to leave is almost never worth the effort.

"Well," she said, to no one.

The gingerbread man ran down the lane and past the farm where the old man was mending a fence. The old man looked up, saw a cookie running, and did what most people do when they see something impossible: he doubted his eyes, then his sanity, then his lunch, and then he gave chase, because chasing is what you do when something doesn't make sense and you want to grab it and hold it still long enough to understand it.

"Run, run, as fast as you can! You can't catch me — I'm the Gingerbread Man!"

The old man ran for about thirty yards and then stopped, hands on his knees, breathing hard, watching the gingerbread man shrink to a speck on the road. He went back to his fence. The fence needed mending regardless of what baked goods were doing.

A cow in the pasture saw the gingerbread man and lumbered after him, because cows will follow anything that moves — it's not predatory, it's just curiosity. Cows are the nosiest animals on earth. They have no agenda. They just want to know what's happening.

"Run, run, as fast as you can!"

The cow stopped after the first hill. Cows are curious but not committed.

A horse in the next field took up the chase — longer stride, more speed, actual competitive instinct. The horse ran hard, mane flying, hooves tearing up the soft earth, and for a moment it seemed like the chase might end here, in a field, with a horse and a cookie and the anticlimax that most stories actually deserve.

But the gingerbread man was faster. Not because a cookie should be faster than a horse — the physics don't support it — but because he was running with something the horse didn't have. The horse was running to catch. The gingerbread man was running to be. Every step was a declaration: I exist, I am here, I am moving, I am free. And that kind of running — running as identity, running as purpose — is faster than anything that's just trying to keep up.

"You can't catch me!"

He believed it. That's the thing. He absolutely, completely, joyfully believed it.

Here is what the gingerbread man understood, in his brief, sweet, impossible life:

He was not meant to last.

He was made of flour and molasses and spice. He was five inches tall. He had raisin eyes that would go stale and frosting that would crack and a body that would soften in the rain and crumble in the cold. He was, by every measure, temporary. A passing thing. A moment with legs.

And he didn't care.

He ran because running was available. He shouted because shouting was available. He existed with the full-throated, unapologetic intensity of something that knows — on some deep, molecular level — that the window is small and the only response to a small window is to throw it all the way open.

You know people like this. Not many — they're rare, the way anything real is rare — but you know them. The ones who walk into a room and the room changes. Not because they're loud or beautiful or important, but because they're there, completely, without reservation, without the part held back that most of us hold back. They're the ones who say what they mean and laugh too loudly and love without calculating the return and leave every room having given it something that wasn't there before.

They don't last, usually. That kind of intensity burns through its container. But while they're here — God. While they're here.

The gingerbread man came to a river.

This was a problem. Rivers are a problem for most people, but for a person made of gingerbread, a river is an existential crisis. Water was the one thing he couldn't outrun. He could feel it — the mist coming off the current, the damp air, the way his edges softened slightly just from being near it. Water wouldn't chase him. It would just dissolve him, calmly and without malice, the way time dissolves everything.

He stood at the bank. Behind him, distantly, the sounds of things still chasing — the old man had given up, but others had joined, a whole parade of pursuers drawn by the absurdity and the smell and the primal need to catch what runs.

A fox appeared.

The fox was sitting on the riverbank, dry and composed, as though she'd been waiting there all along. Which, in a way, she had. Foxes are always waiting. It's their primary skill — not speed, not strength, but the patience to be in the right place when the right moment arrives.

"You seem stuck," said the fox.

"I need to cross," said the gingerbread man.

"I can help with that."

The gingerbread man looked at the fox. The fox looked at the gingerbread man. There was a beat — a small, charged silence in which both of them understood the situation completely and one of them chose to pretend otherwise.

"Climb on my back," said the fox. "I'll swim you across."

"How do I know you won't eat me?"

The fox tilted her head. "Why would I eat you? You're a cookie. I eat mice and rabbits and the occasional unguarded egg. Gingerbread isn't even in my diet."

This was a lie. But it was a good lie — specific, reasonable, delivered with the easy confidence of someone who's been lying professionally for a long time. The gingerbread man wanted to believe it. That's the key. The fox didn't have to be convincing. She just had to offer a version of reality that was more comfortable than the truth, and the gingerbread man — standing at the river's edge, chasers behind him, dissolution in front of him, with nowhere to go and no good options — did what people do when the comfortable lie and the uncomfortable truth are sitting side by side.

He chose the lie.

He climbed on the fox's back.

She waded into the river. The water rose — ankles, knees, belly. "You'd better climb up to my shoulders," she said. "Don't want to get wet." He climbed. The water rose further. "My head, maybe. Just to be safe." He climbed. And then: "My nose. The current's strong here."

He was standing on the tip of the fox's nose, five inches of gingerbread balanced on the point of the one thing he should have run from, and the river was all around him, and the far bank was close, and for one moment — one bright, suspended, impossible moment — he thought he was going to make it.

The fox flicked her head.

The gingerbread man flew upward, briefly, a small arc against the grey sky, turning slowly, catching the light the way something beautiful catches the light, and then he came down, and the fox's mouth was open, and that was that.

One bite. Maybe two. It doesn't matter. What matters is the silence after — the river still flowing, the fox licking her lips, the far bank unreached, and the faint, lingering smell of ginger and cinnamon and clove hanging in the air like the last note of a song.

The fox crossed the river and shook herself dry and continued into the woods. She did not feel guilty. Foxes don't. Guilt requires the belief that you could have done otherwise, and the fox had never, for one second, considered doing otherwise. She was a fox. He was food that talked. The outcome was never in question.

But.

But the gingerbread man had been alive. For twenty minutes, maybe thirty — a half hour of impossible, joyful, shouting, running, sweet-smelling life. And in that half hour, he'd been more alive than most things manage in years. He'd run down a lane and outpaced a horse and taunted every pursuer and felt the wind on his frosting face and known, with absolute certainty, that he was real and he was fast and he was here.

I'm the Gingerbread Man.

Not I'm a gingerbread man. Not I'm one of many. THE Gingerbread Man. The only one. Singular. Unrepeatable. Here and then gone.

The old woman made another batch the following week. Six of them, this time, laid out on the baking sheet in a neat row, raisin eyes staring at the ceiling, three buttons each. She kept the oven door closed. She watched it.

None of them ran.

She ate them with tea, slowly, by the window, and they were good. They were exactly what gingerbread is supposed to be — sweet and warm and spiced and gone in a few bites.

But they weren't him.

Goodnight to the gingerbread man, wherever he went. Goodnight to his raisin eyes and his frosting smile and his five inches of impossible, temporary, magnificent life.

Goodnight to everything that runs — from the oven, from the past, from the thing chasing you, toward the river you can't cross.

Goodnight to the fox, who did what foxes do.

And goodnight to you, who are also temporary, and also here, and also — if you're doing it right — running with everything you've got, shouting your name into the wind, knowing the river's ahead and choosing to run anyway.

You can't catch me.

Nobody can.

Goodnight.

The Lion and the Mouse

Originally by Aesop — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 7 min read 📖 2,178 words 🌙 Best read aloud

The lion was sleeping.

This was not unusual. Lions sleep somewhere between sixteen and twenty hours a day, which sounds like depression but is actually just efficiency. When you are the largest predator on the savanna, when your shoulders are the width of a doorframe and your voice can be heard from five miles away, you don't need to spend a lot of time proving things. You've already proven them. You can rest.

This particular lion slept beneath an acacia tree on the western slope of a hill that had no name. The grass around him was the color of bread crust — golden, dry, warm. His mane fanned out beneath his head like a rough halo. His paws, each one the size of a dinner plate, were crossed in front of him with a delicacy that seemed almost ironic, given what they were capable of.

He was dreaming, probably. Lions dream. Their paws twitch. Their whiskers move. No one knows what they dream about, but it's probably not anything small.

The mouse was not sleeping.

Mice don't have the luxury. When you weigh less than an ounce and approximately seventy percent of the animal kingdom considers you a snack, sleep is a calculated risk. You take it in short bursts, one eye open, ears rotating like satellite dishes, every nap a small act of faith that the world won't eat you in the next four minutes.

This mouse — she was a field mouse, tawny and quick, with black eyes like two wet seeds — was foraging. She moved through the grass the way water moves through cracks: finding every gap, every passage, every route that a larger animal would never notice. The world, at mouse-level, is enormous. A fallen log is a mountain range. A patch of clover is a forest. And a sleeping lion is — well.

She didn't see him until she was on him.

One moment she was navigating the grass, reading the ground with her whiskers, and the next she was climbing something warm and vast and breathing. She'd run up his paw before her brain caught up with her feet. She stopped on the broad plain of his foreleg, looked around, and understood — with the particular clarity that comes right before panic — what she was standing on.

The lion opened one eye.

It was amber. Not metaphorically — actually amber, like the fossilized resin you find in museum gift shops, with depth and light trapped inside it. That eye focused on the mouse with the slow precision of something that has never, in its entire life, needed to rush.

The paw moved.

Not fast. The lion didn't need fast. He simply turned his paw over and the mouse was pinned beneath it, held against the warm earth, surrounded on all sides by pads and claws and the overwhelming, musky, sun-baked smell of lion.

She could feel his heartbeat through his paw. It was slow. Absurdly slow. The resting heart rate of something that feared absolutely nothing.

"Oh," said the mouse, because she couldn't think of anything else.

The lion looked at her. It was not a cruel look. It wasn't much of any look, really. She was so small. She was so unbelievably, comically small. He could close his paw and that would be that, the way you crumple a receipt without thinking, and the effort it would take was so minimal as to barely qualify as effort.

"Please," said the mouse.

The lion blinked.

"Please," she said again. "I'm sorry. I didn't see you. I was — I was going somewhere. I don't even remember where now. Please let me go. I'm not — I'm nothing. I know I'm nothing. But please."

She was shaking. She could feel it and she couldn't stop it. Her whole body was vibrating like a plucked string, fast and thin and desperate.

"If you let me go," she said, "I'll repay you. Someday. I don't know how. I know that sounds ridiculous. I know what you are and I know what I am and I know the math doesn't work. But I will. I promise."

The lion stared at her for a long time. Then something moved across his face — not a smile, lions don't smile, but something adjacent. A flicker. A shift. The faintest possible acknowledgment that this situation, this tiny creature bargaining for her life on the palm of his paw, was something.

He lifted his paw.

The mouse didn't move for a second. She lay there, pressed flat, heart hammering, waiting for the trick. But there was no trick. The paw was up. The sky was visible. She was free.

She ran. She ran so fast she was practically liquid, pouring through the grass, gone in a second, a streak of tawny fur and adrenaline and the overwhelming, dizzying, almost painful relief of being alive when you thought you wouldn't be.

The lion watched her go. Then he put his head back down, closed his eyes, and returned to his dream.

He didn't think about the mouse.

She was too small to think about.

Three weeks passed, or three months. Time in the savanna is measured in seasons and kills and the slow migration of herds across the plain. The lion lived his life, which was the life of a lion: hunting at dusk, sleeping at noon, roaring at things that needed to be roared at. He was magnificent and he knew it, the way mountains know they're tall — not with vanity, just with fact.

The hunters came at dawn.

They were quiet and they were good at what they did and they brought a net. Not a small net — a great, heavy thing woven from rope as thick as a man's thumb, weighted at the edges with stones. They laid it in the grass near the watering hole where the lion drank each morning, and they covered it with dust and leaves, and they waited in the trees with the patience of men who understood that the most dangerous things in the world could be caught, if you were willing to be still long enough.

The lion walked into it without suspecting a thing.

The net came up around him like a hand closing. He roared — a sound that shook leaves from branches and sent birds exploding from the trees in every direction — and he thrashed, and his claws tore at the rope, but the rope held. It was made for this. It had been woven by men who knew what a lion could do, and they'd woven it stronger.

He fought until he couldn't fight. Then he lay in the net, sides heaving, breath coming in great, ragged pulls, and he felt something he hadn't felt since he was a cub too young to know his own strength: helplessness.

The hunters would come back. He knew this the way animals know things — not with logic, but with the deep, gut-level certainty that comes from being a creature in a world full of creatures. They'd come back and they'd take him somewhere, and whatever happened next would not be his to choose.

He lay in the dust, and for the first time in his life, the lion was small.

The mouse heard the roar.

Everyone heard the roar. For miles, every living thing paused — the zebras at the river, the birds in the thornbushes, the beetles in the dirt. A lion's roar is not a sound. It's a weather event. It moves through the ground as much as the air, and your bones hear it before your ears do.

But the mouse heard something in it that the others didn't. She heard the shape of it. Not the power — the fracture. The place where the roar broke, just for a moment, into something that wasn't rage but fear.

She came. It took her a long time. She was a mouse, and the distance was enormous by mouse standards, and she had to cross open ground where hawks circled and snakes waited and the grass was full of things that would eat her without a second thought. But she came.

She found him in the net. He was still. His eyes were open but distant, the way eyes get when the thing behind them has gone somewhere else. Somewhere internal. Somewhere where the math of the situation is being calculated and the numbers aren't good.

"Hey," said the mouse.

The lion focused on her. Recognition took a moment — she was so small, and he had been so big, and the distance between that afternoon under the acacia tree and this morning in the dust was vast.

"Oh," he said. "You."

"Me," she said.

She climbed onto the net. The rope was thick — thicker than she was — but rope is made of fibers, and fibers are made of threads, and threads are made of smaller threads still, and at the level where the mouse lived, everything was made of smaller things that could be taken apart, one piece at a time.

She began to chew.

It was not dramatic. There was no swelling music, no montage. It was just a mouse, chewing rope, in the early morning light, while a lion lay still and watched with an expression that was difficult to read. She chewed through one strand. Then another. Then another. Her teeth were sharp — mouse teeth never stop growing, which means they never stop being sharp, which means a mouse is, in a very specific and very small way, always armed.

It took time. A long time. The sun moved across the sky. The mouse's jaw ached. Her teeth ached. She stopped to rest, and then she went back to it, because she'd promised, and the mouse — unlike many creatures, unlike many people — understood that a promise is not a feeling. It's a decision you make again and again, even when it's hard, even when it hurts, even when the rational part of your brain says this is too much, this is too big, this is not your problem.

The last strand parted.

The net fell open like a flower.

The lion stood. He shook himself — a full-body shake that started at his mane and rippled all the way to his tail — and the dust fell away, and he was himself again. Not diminished. Not reduced. Just free.

He looked down at the mouse. She sat on the remains of the net, panting, jaw sore, covered in rope dust, and she looked back up at him.

Neither of them said anything for a moment.

"The math doesn't work," the lion said, finally. "You said that. That the math doesn't work."

"I remember," said the mouse.

"The math worked fine."

The mouse smiled — or did the mouse equivalent, which is a slight twitching of whiskers that could mean anything but, in this case, meant everything.

They parted ways, as animals do. The lion went back to his hill and his acacia tree and his sixteen hours of sleep. The mouse went back to her grass and her foraging and her small, enormous world.

They didn't become friends, exactly. A lion and a mouse don't have much to talk about on a daily basis. But something connected them now — a thread, invisible and unbreakable, that ran between the hill and the grass like a rope that only they could see.

The lion remembered the mouse. He thought about her sometimes, usually in the late afternoon, when the light was gold and the savanna was quiet and the world seemed, for a moment, gentle enough to think in.

He thought about how small she was. And how that hadn't mattered.

He thought about how the biggest, strongest, most magnificent creature on the plain had been saved by the smallest. And how the smallest had saved him not with strength — she had none — but with patience, and persistence, and a promise she didn't have to keep but kept anyway.

And the mouse, in her nest of grass and stolen cotton, thought about the lion too. She thought about the weight of his paw, and the amber of his eye, and the moment he'd lifted that paw and let her go — not because she could help him, but because squashing her wasn't worth the effort.

Mercy, given carelessly. Repaid with everything she had.

That's the story. That's all of it.

Be kind to small things. Not because they'll save you someday — that's a bonus, not the point. Be kind to small things because kindness costs you nothing and cruelty costs you everything, and you never know which small thing is carrying a promise it fully intends to keep.

Goodnight, lion. Sleep your sixteen hours. You've earned them.

Goodnight, mouse. Keep your teeth sharp. Keep your promises sharper.

Goodnight.

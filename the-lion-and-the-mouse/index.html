The Lion and the Mouse

Originally by Aesop — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 7 min read 📖 769 words 🌙 Best read aloud

The lion was sleeping. This was not unusual. Lions sleep sixteen to twenty hours a day, which sounds like depression but is just efficiency. When you're the biggest thing on the savanna, you don't need to spend much time proving it.

He slept under an acacia tree. Golden grass all around. Paws crossed in front of him with a delicacy that seemed ironic, given what they could do.

The mouse was not sleeping. Mice can't afford to. When most of the animal kingdom considers you a snack, every nap is a small act of faith.

She was foraging — tawny and quick, black eyes like wet seeds — moving through the grass the way water moves through cracks. At mouse-level, the world is enormous. A fallen log is a mountain. A sleeping lion is—

She didn't see him until she was on him.

Up his paw before her brain caught up. She stopped on his foreleg and understood what she was standing on.

He opened one eye. Amber. Like actual amber, with light trapped inside.

His paw turned over. She was pinned. Surrounded by pads and claws and the overwhelming smell of sun-baked lion.

She could feel his heartbeat through his paw. Slow. Absurdly slow.

"Please," she said. "I'm sorry. I was going somewhere. I don't even remember where. Please let me go."

She was shaking. Vibrating like a plucked string.

"If you let me go, I'll repay you someday. I know that sounds ridiculous. I know the math doesn't work. But I will."

The lion looked at her for a long time. Then something crossed his face — not a smile, but something close. He lifted his paw.

She ran. So fast she was practically liquid.

The lion put his head back down and went back to sleep. He didn't think about the mouse. She was too small to think about.

Weeks passed. The lion lived his life — hunting at dusk, sleeping at noon, roaring at things that needed roaring at.

The hunters came at dawn. Quiet, skilled, with a net. Heavy rope, thick as a man's thumb, weighted with stones. They laid it by the watering hole, covered it with dust, and waited.

The lion walked into it.

The net came up around him. He roared — a sound that shook leaves from trees — and he thrashed, and his claws tore at the rope, but the rope held. It was made for this.

He fought until he couldn't. Then he lay there, sides heaving, and felt something he hadn't felt since he was a cub: helplessness.

The mouse heard the roar. Everyone did. But she heard something in it the others didn't. Not power — fracture. The place where it broke into something that wasn't rage but fear.

She came. It took a long time. She had to cross open ground where hawks circled and snakes waited. But she came.

She found him in the net. Eyes open but distant.

"Hey," she said.

He focused. Recognition took a moment.

"Oh. You."

"Me."

She climbed onto the net. The rope was thick, but rope is made of fibers, and fibers are made of threads, and at mouse-level, everything can be taken apart one piece at a time.

She chewed. One strand. Then another. Her teeth ached. She stopped to rest, then went back, because she'd promised. And a promise isn't a feeling — it's a decision you make again and again, even when it hurts.

The last strand parted. The net fell open.

The lion stood. Shook himself — dust flying — and was himself again.

He looked down at the mouse. She sat on the ruined net, panting, jaw sore.

"The math doesn't work," he said. "That's what you told me."

"I remember."

"The math worked fine."

They went their separate ways. But something connected them now — invisible, unbreakable.

The lion thought about her sometimes, in the late afternoon. How small she was. How that hadn't mattered.

And the mouse thought about the lion. The weight of his paw. The moment he'd lifted it — not because she could help him, but because squashing her wasn't worth the effort.

Mercy, given carelessly. Repaid with everything she had.

Be kind to small things. Not because they'll save you — that's a bonus, not the point. Be kind because kindness costs nothing and you never know which small thing is carrying a promise it fully intends to keep.

Goodnight, lion. Goodnight, mouse.

Goodnight.

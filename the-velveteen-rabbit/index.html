The Velveteen Rabbit

Originally by Margery Williams — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 10 min read 📖 530 words 🌙 Best read aloud

He arrived on Christmas morning, stuffed into a stocking between an orange and a bag of chocolate coins. Brown velveteen, white spots, stitched whiskers, shiny button eyes. He was soft and new and his fur smelled like the shop.

The boy pulled him out, held him up, smiled. Then the other presents came — a wooden train, a set of tin soldiers, a picture book — and the rabbit went on a shelf in the nursery.

He sat there between a mechanical mouse and a model boat, and he waited.

The nursery was full of toys, and the expensive ones were very proud of themselves. The mechanical mouse talked about his gears. The model boat described its rigging in great detail. The tin soldiers stood at attention and said nothing, which was its own kind of showing off.

The Skin Horse had been in the nursery longer than any of them. He was old the way loved things get old — smooth at the joints, mane mostly rubbed away, one ear loose, his tail long gone. The newer toys looked at him with a kind of embarrassed pity. He didn't seem to mind.

One evening, the Velveteen Rabbit found himself next to the Skin Horse and asked the question he'd been turning over since Christmas.

"What is Real?" he asked. "Does it mean having things that buzz inside you and a stick-out handle?"

The Skin Horse smiled — or as close to smiling as a toy horse can manage. "Real isn't how you are made," he said. "It's a thing that happens to you. When a child loves you for a long, long time — not just to play with, but really loves you — then you become Real."

"Does it hurt?" asked the Rabbit.

"Sometimes," said the Skin Horse. He was always truthful. "When you are Real, you don't mind being hurt."

"Does it happen all at once, like being wound up? Or bit by bit?"

"It doesn't happen all at once. You become. It takes a long time. That's why it doesn't often happen to people who break easily, or have sharp edges, or who have to be carefully kept. Generally, by the time you are Real, most of your hair has been loved off, and your eyes drop out, and you get loose in the joints and very shabby."

"But these things don't matter at all," said the Skin Horse, "because once you are Real, you can't be ugly. Except to people who don't understand."

The Rabbit thought about this for a long time. He looked at his own shiny button eyes and his fresh velveteen fur and he wondered what it would feel like to be shabby and loved instead of new and forgotten.

One night, the boy's nanny was in a hurry. The boy was crying for his bedtime toy and she couldn't find it anywhere, so she grabbed the Velveteen Rabbit off the shelf and tucked him into the boy's arms.

"Here — take your bunny."

That was it. That was the beginning of everything.

The boy's arms closed around him. Warm. Tight. The kind of hold where you can feel someone's heartbeat through the fabric.

After that, the Rabbit slept in the boy's bed every night. In the crook of his arm, against his chest, the boy's warmth soaking into the velveteen. The boy talked to him before sleep — told him things, the way children do, as if a stuffed rabbit were the most trustworthy confidant in the world.

He took him to the garden. He took him to the beach. He propped him up at the breakfast table and poured him imaginary tea.

"He's not a toy," the boy told anyone who asked. "He's Real."

The Rabbit changed. Slowly. The velveteen wore thin where the boy held tightest — around the belly, along the back. His whiskers came loose. One button eye hung by a thread. His stuffing shifted and bunched, so he was fatter in some places and flatter in others. His fur, which had been brown and glossy at Christmas, became grey and patchy.

He didn't look like a new toy anymore. He looked like someone's rabbit. And that was better.

The Skin Horse looked at him from across the nursery and nodded. He recognized what was happening.

Spring came. The boy carried the Rabbit outside and set him in the grass at the edge of the garden.

Two wild rabbits came out of the bracken. They hopped over and looked at him — this shabby, stuffed thing sitting perfectly still in the grass.

"Why doesn't he move?" said one rabbit to the other.

They came closer. Sniffed him. Nudged him with their noses.

"Why don't you hop?" they asked. "Why don't you run?"

"I don't know how," said the Velveteen Rabbit.

They wrinkled their noses and hopped away. Everything about them was alive in a way he wasn't — the twitch of their ears, the spring in their legs, the way they moved through the grass like water. He sat there and felt, for the first time, what he couldn't do.

But the boy came back and scooped him up, and the feeling faded. Being loved was enough. It had always been enough.

Then the boy got sick.

Scarlet fever. Bad. His face was flushed and his skin was hot and he shook at night. The Rabbit was right there the whole time — pressed against his cheek, damp with sweat, catching whispered words that didn't quite make sense. The boy held him tighter when the fever was worst, the way you hold something when you're afraid of the dark.

The fever broke. The boy got better. The doctor came and said the room had to be cleaned — everything burned. The sheets. The pillows. The curtains.

The toys.

They put the Velveteen Rabbit in a sack with the old bedding and carried him out to the garden, to the pile by the bonfire, and left him there.

He lay in the sack in the dark. He wasn't angry. He was just sad. He thought about the boy's arms and the warmth and the whispered conversations and the way the boy had said he's Real to anyone who'd listen.

Was he Real? The Skin Horse had said it took a long time. He'd been loved for a long time. His fur was rubbed off. His eyes were loose. He was shabby and worn and full of the boy's warmth.

Something fell from his button eye. A real tear — the first one. It rolled down his velveteen face and dropped onto the ground.

And where it fell, a flower grew. Right up through the earth — green stem, golden petals, opening in the moonlight. And standing in the flower was someone. Small, glowing, gentle.

"I am the nursery magic Fairy," she said. "I take care of toys that children have loved. When they are done being needed, I make them Real."

"But I am Real," said the Rabbit. "The boy said so."

"He made you Real to him," said the Fairy. "Now I'll make you Real to everyone."

She kissed him on his shabby velveteen nose. And something happened.

His legs worked. Not the floppy, stuffing-filled legs — real legs, with muscles and tendons and fur that grew from skin instead of fabric. His nose twitched. His ears turned. He could smell the grass, the earth, the night air. He could hear the crickets and the owl and the wind in the trees.

He hopped. One tentative hop. Then another. Then he was running — actually running, through the grass, under the moonlight, his back legs pushing off the earth the way the wild rabbits' legs had, and it was nothing like sitting still and it was everything he'd ever wanted.

The wild rabbits found him in the meadow. They didn't look confused this time.

"You can move," they said.

"I can move."

"You can hop."

"I can hop."

They hopped together under the moon. Three rabbits in a field, and you couldn't tell which one used to be velveteen.

Years later, the boy — taller now, older — was walking through the field near his old house. A brown and white rabbit sat in the grass and watched him pass. There was something about it. Something familiar. The way it held its head. The way it didn't quite run away.

"That one," said the boy, "looks just like my old Bunny."

The rabbit watched him go. He remembered everything — the stocking, the shelf, the nursery, the Skin Horse's voice, the boy's arms, the warmth, the fever, the sack, the tear, the flower. All of it.

Then the boy walked on. And the rabbit hopped away. And the field was quiet.

Goodnight to the Velveteen Rabbit, who became Real.

Goodnight to the Skin Horse, who knew how it worked all along.

Goodnight to the boy, who loved something until it was true.

Goodnight to all the shabby, worn, much-loved things on the shelf and in the drawer and in the box in the closet. You were Real. You still are.

Goodnight.

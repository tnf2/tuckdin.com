Sleeping Beauty

Originally by Charles Perrault / Brothers Grimm — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 10 min read 📖 2,377 words 🌙 Best read aloud

The curse was given at a party, which is where most curses are given, if you think about it.

A christening party, to be specific. The king and queen had a daughter — finally, after years of wanting, after doctors and prayers and the slow, corrosive patience of people who keep hoping for something that doesn't come. And then it came. She came. Small and pink and screaming, the way all new things are: loud and helpless and completely unaware of the machinery they've set in motion.

They invited everyone to the christening. Everyone except one.

Now, the story calls her a fairy, and the other guests were fairies too — twelve of them, one for each place setting — but fairy is just the word for someone with power in a world where power isn't supposed to exist. Call them what you want. Influential women. Family friends with strong opinions. The twelve aunts who show up at christenings and give the baby gifts that are really projections of their own values: beauty, grace, wit, talent, a good singing voice, the ability to dance. Each fairy stepped forward and bestowed her gift on the sleeping child like a benediction, like a resume being built before the interview.

The thirteenth fairy was not invited.

Her name was Carabosse, and the not-inviting was not an accident. It was a decision. She'd been left off the list deliberately, consciously, the way people are left off lists — not because they're forgotten, but because they're inconvenient. Carabosse was difficult. She said things people didn't want to hear. She arrived at parties and the temperature dropped, not because she was cold but because she was honest, and honesty in a room full of pleasantries has the same effect as a window opening in winter.

They didn't invite her because she would have ruined the mood.

She came anyway.

The door opened, and there she was — tall, thin, dressed in black not for drama but because black was what she wore, and she walked to the cradle and looked down at the baby with an expression that was not anger, exactly, but something older and colder. The expression of someone who has been excluded and has decided to make the exclusion meaningful.

"Before her sixteenth birthday," Carabosse said, "she will prick her finger on a spindle. And she will die."

The room went silent. The kind of silence that has weight — you can feel it pressing on your chest, on your throat, on the part of your brain that's scrambling to process what it just heard and can't.

Carabosse left.

The twelfth fairy — the one who hadn't given her gift yet — stepped forward. She was young, and her hands were shaking, and she didn't have enough power to undo the curse entirely, because curses given from genuine hurt are harder to break than curses given from malice, and Carabosse's hurt was genuine.

"Not death," the twelfth fairy said. "Sleep. A deep sleep, for a hundred years, until she is woken by—"

She paused. The room waited.

"By the right person. At the right time."

It was the best she could do.

The king did what kings do: he tried to control the uncontrollable. He ordered every spindle in the kingdom destroyed. Every spinning wheel, every loom, every pointed thing that could conceivably prick a finger was confiscated, burned, banned. He built a world without spindles the way anxious parents build worlds without risk — thoroughly, desperately, and with the fundamental misunderstanding that removing the object removes the danger.

The princess grew. She was given a name — Aurora, or Briar Rose, or Rose, depending on who's telling the story — and the gifts the fairies had given her unfolded on schedule: beauty, grace, wit, a singing voice, all of it arriving with the reliable precision of a subscription service. She was everything they'd wished for, which meant she was a composite of other people's desires, which is a strange thing to be.

She was happy, in the way that people are happy when they don't know what they're missing. She lived in a castle where every sharp thing had been removed, where every corner was padded, where every risk had been anticipated and neutralized. It was safe. It was beautiful. It was the architectural equivalent of a life lived entirely in bubble wrap.

She never saw a spindle. She never pricked her finger. For fifteen years, the king's plan worked perfectly.

On the morning of her sixteenth birthday, Aurora found a door she'd never noticed before.

This is the moment. Not the spindle, not the sleep — the door. Because every controlled environment has a door that hasn't been found yet, a room that wasn't accounted for, a gap in the bubble wrap. And the person inside — the person who's been kept safe, who's been wrapped and padded and protected from every sharp thing — that person will always, eventually, find the door. Not because they're rebellious. Because they're alive. And alive things seek what they haven't encountered yet, the way roots seek water and light seeks cracks and teenagers seek exactly the thing you told them to avoid.

Behind the door: a staircase. At the top of the staircase: a room. In the room: an old woman, spinning.

The old woman was not Carabosse. She was just an old woman with a spinning wheel, doing what she'd always done, tucked away in a tower that the king's purge had somehow missed. She looked up at Aurora with mild surprise and the absolute lack of alarm that comes from being eighty and having seen everything.

"What's that?" Aurora asked, pointing at the spindle.

"Come and see," said the old woman.

Aurora reached out. She touched the spindle. The point found her finger, and a single drop of blood welled up — bright, red, the first sharp thing her body had ever encountered — and her eyes closed.

She didn't fall. She sank. Slowly, gracefully, the way things sink in water — not a collapse but a surrender, her body giving itself to something it had been moving toward since the day the curse was spoken. She sank to the floor and her breathing slowed and her face relaxed into an expression of such profound peace that the old woman, watching, felt not horror but envy.

To sleep like that. To stop.

The sleep spread.

It moved through the castle like a gentle tide — down the stairs, through the halls, into every room. The king slept. The queen slept. The cooks slept, the guards slept, the horses in the stable slept standing up. The fire in the kitchen slept, frozen mid-flame. The flies on the wall slept. The wind slept. Time itself seemed to pause, to hold its breath, to decide that this particular moment would last as long as it needed to.

Thorns grew.

They rose around the castle like a living wall — thick, dark, impenetrable, covered in roses that bloomed regardless of season. The thorns were not decoration. They were defense. They were the physical manifestation of the thing that happens when someone retreats from the world: the world grows over them, fills in the space where they were, makes it harder and harder to get back in.

A hundred years is a long time.

Long enough for the castle to become a story. For the story to become a legend. For the legend to become a fairy tale that parents told children at bedtime — there's a princess in there, behind the thorns, sleeping, waiting — and the children would press their faces to the window and look at the dark tangle of roses and thorns and wonder.

People tried to get through. Young men, mostly — princes, knights, boys with swords and certainty and the particular confidence that comes from never having been told no. They hacked at the thorns. The thorns grew back. They climbed. The thorns pulled them down. Some died. The thorns didn't care. Thorns are indifferent to ambition.

A hundred years passed.

The prince who finally came was not special. He was not stronger or braver or more worthy than the others. He was just on time. The hundred years were up. The curse had run its course. The thorns parted for him the way doors open when you have an appointment — not because of who you are, but because of when you arrive.

He walked through. The roses brushed his shoulders. The castle appeared, grey and still, covered in ivy and silence, everything exactly as it had been the moment the sleep began.

He found her in the tower room, on the floor beside the spinning wheel. Her hair had grown — a hundred years of hair, spread across the stone like a dark river. Her face was unchanged. Sleep is like that — it preserves you, holds you in amber, keeps you exactly as you were at the moment you stopped.

He knelt beside her. He didn't know what to do. The legend said a kiss, and a kiss is a simple thing, and simple things feel insufficient in the face of a hundred years of sleep. But he knelt, and he looked at her face, and he felt something that was not love — not yet, you can't love someone you've never met — but the precursor to love. The willingness to begin.

He kissed her.

Her eyes opened.

Not suddenly. Slowly. The way you open your eyes after a long sleep — confused, disoriented, the world coming in too bright and too much. She blinked. She looked at him. She looked at the room. She looked at the spinning wheel, and the window, and the ivy growing through the stones, and the light, and the hundred years sat between them like an ocean she'd crossed without knowing she was swimming.

"How long?" she said.

"A hundred years."

She was quiet for a moment. A long moment. Processing a century's worth of absence in the space between two breaths.

"Everyone I knew," she said.

"Yes."

"My parents—"

"They're here. They slept too. Everyone slept."

She closed her eyes. Opened them. The relief was so large it didn't look like relief — it looked like pain, the way very bright light looks like pain when you've been in the dark too long.

The castle woke. The fire resumed burning. The flies resumed flying. The cook resumed cooking, picking up mid-sentence, mid-stir, as if a hundred years were a hiccup, a blink, an interruption so brief it barely registered.

But it had registered. The world outside was different. A hundred years of different. The language had shifted. The roads had changed. The people Aurora had known — friends, neighbors, the baker who gave her extra rolls — were not just gone but layered over, buried under generations of living that had happened without her.

She stood at the window and looked out at a world she didn't recognize, and she felt the particular vertigo of someone who has been paused while everything else played on. Like falling asleep on a train and waking up in a different city. Like going away and coming back and finding that back no longer exists.

The prince stood beside her. He didn't try to fix it. He didn't say it'll be fine or you'll adjust or any of the optimistic things people say when they can't bear someone else's grief. He just stood there, present, the way the twelfth fairy had promised: the right person, at the right time.

"Tell me about it," Aurora said. "Tell me about the hundred years."

And he did. He told her about the world she'd missed — the wars and the inventions and the music and the foolish things and the beautiful things and the ordinary things that make up a century of human living. He told her slowly, a piece at a time, the way you feed someone who hasn't eaten in a long time — carefully, gently, not too much at once.

And she listened. And the world outside the window became, slowly, not less strange, but less frightening. Not home, but somewhere she could learn to live.

Here's what the story is actually about:

Not the prince. Not the kiss. Not the curse.

It's about the sleep.

The long, deep, involuntary retreat from a world that's too sharp, too fast, too full of spindles. The closing of the eyes. The growing of thorns. The decision — or the non-decision, because sleep is never really a choice — to stop participating. To pause. To let the ivy grow over you while the world moves on.

You've done this. Not for a hundred years. But for a season, maybe. A year. The period after the loss, after the failure, after the thing that pricked you. The time when you pulled the covers up and the world grew thorns around you and the people who tried to reach you couldn't get through — not because they weren't brave enough, but because it wasn't time yet.

The sleep ends when the sleep ends. Not when someone kisses you. Not when the right prince arrives. The prince is just the first thing you see when you open your eyes, and you could just as easily see a sunrise or a dog or a cup of coffee or your own face in the mirror, and any of them could be the thing that says: okay. It's time. You can wake up now.

The thorns part when you're ready. Not before.

Goodnight to Aurora, who slept because she had to and woke because she could.

Goodnight to the thirteenth fairy, who cursed a baby because no one invited her to the party, and who was wrong to do it, and who was right to be hurt.

Goodnight to the thorns, which were terrible and necessary.

And goodnight to you, awake or asleep or somewhere in between. The spindle's been touched. The sleep has been slept. The eyes are opening.

Welcome back.

Goodnight.

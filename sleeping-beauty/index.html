Sleeping Beauty

Originally by Charles Perrault / Brothers Grimm — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 10 min read 📖 916 words 🌙 Best read aloud

The curse was given at a party. Which is where most curses are given, if you think about it.

A christening. The king and queen finally had a daughter — after years of wanting, doctors, prayers. They invited everyone. Twelve fairies, one for each place setting. Each stepped forward and gave the baby a gift: beauty, grace, wit, a singing voice. A resume being built before the interview.

The thirteenth fairy wasn't invited. Her name was Carabosse. Left off the list deliberately — not forgotten, but inconvenient. She was difficult. She said things people didn't want to hear. She arrived at parties and the temperature dropped. Not because she was cold, but because she was honest, and honesty among pleasantries hits like a window opening in winter.

She came anyway.

"Before her sixteenth birthday, she will prick her finger on a spindle. And she will die."

The twelfth fairy hadn't given her gift yet. Shaking hands. Not enough power to undo the curse — curses from genuine hurt are harder to break than curses from malice.

"Not death. Sleep. A deep sleep, for a hundred years. Until she is woken by the right person, at the right time."

Best she could do.

The king destroyed every spindle in the kingdom. Every spinning wheel, loom, pointed thing. He built a world without spindles the way anxious parents build worlds without risk — thoroughly, desperately, missing the fundamental point.

The princess grew. Aurora, or Briar Rose. The fairy gifts unfolded on schedule. She was everything they'd wished for — a composite of other people's desires.

She was happy the way people are happy when they don't know what they're missing. A castle where every sharp thing was removed. Every corner padded. Every risk neutralized. Beautiful. Safe. Bubble wrap.

On her sixteenth birthday, she found a door she'd never noticed.

Every controlled environment has one. A gap in the padding. And the person inside will always find it. Not from rebellion — from being alive. Alive things seek what they haven't encountered.

Behind the door: a staircase. At the top: a room. An old woman, spinning. Not Carabosse — just an old woman the purge had missed.

"What's that?"

"Come and see."

Aurora touched the spindle. The point found her finger. One bright drop of blood — the first sharp thing she'd ever met.

Her eyes closed. She didn't fall — she sank. Slowly, gracefully. Like something surrendering.

The sleep spread. Through the castle like a tide. The king, the queen, the cooks, the guards, the horses. Fire frozen mid-flame. Time itself held its breath.

Thorns grew around the castle. Thick, dark, covered in roses. Not decoration — defense. What happens when someone retreats from the world: the world grows over them.

A hundred years passed. The castle became a story, then a legend, then a fairy tale told at bedtime. People tried to get through — young men with swords and certainty. The thorns pulled them down. Thorns don't care about ambition.

The prince who came was not special. Not stronger or braver. Just on time. The hundred years were up. The thorns parted for him the way doors open when you have an appointment.

He found her in the tower room. Hair grown to a dark river across the stone. Face unchanged — sleep preserves you in amber.

He kissed her. Not because a kiss is sufficient for a hundred years of sleep, but because it was the beginning of something.

Her eyes opened. Slowly. The way you open them after a long sleep — confused, the world too bright.

"How long?"

"A hundred years."

Quiet. Processing a century in two breaths.

"Everyone I knew—"

"They're here. They slept too."

Relief so large it looked like pain.

The castle woke. Fire resumed. Flies resumed. The cook picked up mid-sentence.

But the world outside was different. Language shifted. Roads changed. The people she'd known — buried under generations.

Aurora stood at the window, looking at a world she didn't recognize. The vertigo of being paused while everything played on.

The prince stood beside her. He didn't say it'll be fine. He just stood there.

"Tell me about it," she said. "The hundred years."

He did. Slowly. A piece at a time. The way you feed someone who hasn't eaten in a long time.

It's about the sleep.

The long retreat from a world that's too sharp. The closing of eyes. The thorns growing. The decision — or non-decision — to stop participating.

You've done this. Not a hundred years. A season. A year. The time after the loss, when you pulled the covers up and the thorns grew and the people who tried to reach you couldn't get through. Not because they weren't good enough — because it wasn't time yet.

The sleep ends when it ends. Not when someone kisses you. The prince is just the first thing you see when you open your eyes. Could just as easily be a sunrise, or a dog, or your own face in the mirror.

The thorns part when you're ready. Not before.

Goodnight to Aurora, who slept because she had to and woke because she could.

Goodnight to the thorns — terrible and necessary.

And goodnight to you, awake or asleep or somewhere in between. The eyes are opening.

Welcome back.

Goodnight.

The Boy Who Cried Wolf

Originally by Aesop — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 7 min read 📖 728 words 🌙 Best read aloud

There was a boy. Call him whatever you like — Thomas, Lucas, that kid from down the road with grass stains on his elbows and a look like he was planning something.

He was maybe thirteen. Old enough to be given a job. Young enough to hate it.

The job was: watch the sheep.

Sheep aren't complicated. They eat. They stand near each other. Sometimes one wanders off and you go get it. That's the whole thing. A dog could do it. The dog seemed to enjoy it. That made the boy feel worse.

The village sat between two hills that turned purple at dusk. Everyone knew everyone. The blacksmith was Harmon. The baker was Elda. The mayor was a woman named Greta who'd been mayor so long the word basically became her first name.

The boy's father was a shepherd. His grandfather too. Same hills, same grass, same sheep. The adults found this comforting. The boy found it suffocating.

He wanted something to happen.

You know the feeling. You've had it at a desk, in a meeting, on a Tuesday that wouldn't end.

So one afternoon — blue sky, quiet hills, sheep doing nothing — he stood on the wall and shouted:

"WOLF! WOLF!"

The village came running. Harmon with a hammer. Elda with a rolling pin. Mayor Greta with a walking stick she held like she meant business.

No wolf.

The boy sat on the wall, grinning. "False alarm."

"Don't do that," said Greta, in a voice with corners.

He wasn't sorry. For five minutes, he'd been the center of everything. The whole village had come running for him. It was the most alive he'd felt in months.

He did it again a week later.

They came slower. But they came. Because what if.

No wolf.

Harmon stared at him. "Boy. If you do this again, we won't come."

The boy felt something cold at the base of his spine. But the warmth was still there too — the attention, the rush. And warmth is louder than cold. At first.

Autumn. The hills turned brown. The sheep grew thick coats.

The wolf came out of the trees at the far end of the pasture. Grey — not storybook grey. Wet stone grey. It moved with a patience that was worse than speed.

The sheep bunched together. A low bleat rose from them. Not loud — just urgent.

The boy's mouth went dry. His legs were shaking.

"WOLF! PLEASE! WOLF!"

Down in the village, Harmon turned back to his anvil. Elda wiped her hands. Greta looked up from her reading, listened, and went back to it. Someone laughed.

The boy screamed until his voice cracked. Nobody came.

The wolf took three sheep. It could have taken more. It didn't. Wolves have a sense of enough.

He came down the hill at dusk. Three fewer sheep. A look on his face the village hadn't seen before.

Greta met him at the bottom. She didn't say I told you so.

"We heard you," she said.

"I know."

"We didn't believe you."

"I know."

She put her hand on his shoulder. Not gentle. Steady. The hand of someone who'd run a village for thirty years.

"Trust is like a rope," she said. "You can cut it in a second. Braiding it back takes time. And it's never quite as strong where the cut was."

He went back to the hill the next day. And the next. He watched the sheep. He didn't shout. He just did the work.

Slowly, the village began to trust him again. Not the easy kind — the kind you give by default. The rebuilt kind. Heavier, rougher, more valuable.

He grew up. Became a shepherd, like his father. The sheep were still boring. The hills were still the same.

But he understood now. Words aren't just sounds. They're promises. Every time you speak, you're asking someone to believe you. And belief is a gift you can't buy back once you've wasted it.

He never lied about wolves again.

Though on certain autumn evenings, he'd see something move in the shadows — low and grey — and feel that cold at the base of his spine.

And he'd handle it himself.

Goodnight.

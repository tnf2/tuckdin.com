The Fox and the Grapes

Originally by Aesop — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 6 min read 📖 648 words 🌙 Best read aloud

The fox wasn't hungry. She'd had a fine breakfast — two eggs and some blackberries from the hedge. She was comfortable.

She just wanted the grapes.

They hung from an arbor at the edge of an old vineyard. Dark purple, dusty bloom on the skin. The kind of ripe where you know the first bite will be an event. Sweet with an edge. Warm from the sun.

She wanted them the way you want things that aren't necessary. Not with the body. With the mind.

She jumped. Good jump — foxes are excellent jumpers. Her claws brushed the lowest leaf. She could smell them.

She missed. Landed neatly, because foxes always land neatly. That's non-negotiable.

She tried again. Running start this time. Higher. Closer. Her teeth were inches from the nearest cluster.

Air.

Landed neatly. Of course.

She tried from the crumbling wall. Wrong angle. She scrambled, slipped, caught herself. Stones clattered down.

A magpie was watching from a fence post. Magpies always watch.

The fox did not acknowledge the magpie.

One more try. The best yet — explosive, precise. For a half-second she hung in the air, jaws closing on—

Nothing. Grape-scented air.

Landed neatly.

She sat down. Licked her paw. Not because it needed licking — because she needed a moment.

And something shifted inside her. A small, quiet door closing. The wanting went dull. Something protective wrapped itself around the disappointment like a bandage.

She looked up at the grapes.

"Probably sour," she said. Out loud. To no one. "Green. Not worth the effort."

She turned and walked away. Not fast — that would suggest retreat. Slowly, deliberately. Tail high. Every line of her body saying I'm fine.

She knew they weren't sour. She was a fox — her nose could detect a rabbit in a burrow from forty yards. She knew exactly what those grapes were. Perfect.

And she told herself they were sour anyway.

Not because she was stupid. Because she was smart. Smart enough to build a story that explained away the failure. Smart enough to furnish a little house of logic around the hurt and move right in.

I didn't fail. The thing wasn't worth having.

You've done this. Not with grapes. But with something.

A job you didn't get — would've been soul-crushing anyway. A person who didn't call back — weren't that interesting. A dream you stopped chasing — impractical, childish.

It's the most natural thing in the world. The mind protects itself — wraps the bruise before you've registered the pain. You say sour and walk away and don't feel the loss.

Because the sentence you can't say is: I tried my best and it wasn't enough.

That's the one the fox was running from.

The grapes went on being grapes. A bird ate them eventually. The fox went home to her den, curled up, nose under tail.

She did not think about grapes.

She was very firm about this.

But sometimes, on warm afternoons, when the breeze carried something sweet from the vineyard, she'd pause. Just a flicker.

And she'd wonder — not about the grapes. About the door that closed. Whether protecting yourself from disappointment is the same as protecting yourself from wanting. Whether a life without reaching and missing is safe, or just small.

She never answered the question. Foxes don't.

But on the quietest nights, in the part of her mind where no magpie could see, she'd admit the grapes were probably wonderful.

And that she wished she could have reached them.

Goodnight, fox. Goodnight, grapes.

Goodnight to all the things we wanted and couldn't reach, and the stories we told ourselves to make the walking away easier.

They were never sour. But it's okay that we said they were.

Goodnight.

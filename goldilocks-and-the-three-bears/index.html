Goldilocks and the Three Bears

Originally by Robert Southey — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 8 min read 📖 962 words 🌙 Best read aloud

The bears had a system.

Most families do. Unspoken agreements about who sits where, how the morning works. Theirs was simple: Papa made the porridge — thick, the spoon stood up, same recipe his mother used. Mama set the table — three bowls, three spoons, three chairs at precise angles nobody agreed on but everyone maintained. Baby Bear sat and waited.

He was nine. He'd asked several times if they could have eggs. These requests were met with the polite, absolute silence parents deploy when a suggestion has been heard and denied without the formality of an actual denial.

The porridge was always too hot. Law of physics. So every morning they went for a walk while it cooled.

They left the door unlocked, because they lived in the woods and nobody locks their door in the woods. This was their first mistake, though calling it a mistake implies they should have known. Trust is only wrong in hindsight.

Her name was Eleanor. Everyone called her Goldilocks because of the hair — so blonde it caught every bit of light — and the nickname stuck the way nicknames do.

She was nineteen. Walking through the woods because she'd had a fight with her mother about something that seemed important now and wouldn't by Thursday. She'd said something she'd regret. Her mother said something she'd regret. Eleanor walked out, because walking out is what nineteen-year-olds do when they don't have the words yet for I'm sorry or I'm scared.

She walked until the anger cooled. Then she kept walking because the rhythm felt good.

She found the cottage by accident. Wooden, well-kept, flower boxes. The door was open. She pushed it.

She shouldn't have. She knew that. But the house was warm and empty and smelled like breakfast, and there's a kind of loneliness that makes other people's spaces feel like invitations.

Three bowls of porridge. First one — too hot, burned her tongue. Second — cold, room temperature, which is no temperature for porridge. Third — just right.

Not a phrase that gets enough credit. Just right sounds like settling. But it's the hardest thing to find. Too hot is easy. Too cold is easy. The place in the middle where the thing meets you exactly where you are — that's rare.

She ate the whole bowl.

Three chairs. The first was enormous — deep, wide. She felt lost in it. Like a job too big for you. The second was too soft — so yielding she disappeared into it. Like a relationship where the other person agrees with everything.

The third fit. Her edges touched its edges. She leaned back. Sighed.

It broke. One moment resting, the next on the floor in broken wood.

That's the thing about just right. Sometimes it can't hold you. Fitting and lasting aren't the same thing.

Upstairs. Three beds. First — too hard, every bone complained. Second — too soft, she sank in and felt trapped. Third — just right. Her body relaxed all at once, a full exhale. She slept the way you sleep when you've been fighting something and finally stop.

The bears came home. They knew immediately — not from the porridge or the chair, but because the house felt different. A space occupied by a stranger carries that charge.

Papa went first. Down the hall, to the bedroom.

There she was. Curled on her side, one hand under the pillow.

She woke. Three bears filling the doorway. Papa was large in the way nature is large — without effort. Mama stood behind him with an expression that was anger and curiosity and something like recognition. Baby Bear was peering between them, wide-eyed. Most interesting thing that has ever happened during breakfast.

Eleanor didn't scream. The bears weren't moving.

"I'm sorry," she said, and the words came before she could think, the ones she'd been unable to find that morning. "I'm so sorry. I broke the chair. I ate the porridge. I shouldn't be here."

She was crying. Not dramatically — leaking. The fight, the walking, the loneliness that led her through a stranger's door.

Mama Bear sat on the edge of the bed and put her paw on Eleanor's shoulder. Heavy in a way that wasn't threatening — grounding. Like a weighted blanket pressing you into the present.

"You ate his porridge," she said, nodding at Baby Bear.

"I know. I'm sorry."

"He's been wanting eggs anyway."

Baby Bear's face lit up. Papa Bear's did something complicated.

They made her tea. She sat at their table — too big for her, but not in a bad way. She told them about the fight. Not the details — the shape. Being nineteen and not knowing where you fit. Trying every chair and bed and bowl and finding nothing quite right, or that the things that fit break under you.

"Everything is 'just right' for about five minutes," Mama Bear said. "Then you grow, or it shrinks. That's not a problem. That's just life."

Eleanor walked home. She said the things she'd been unable to say. Her mother listened. Then made porridge — not because it was perfect, but because it was what she had.

It wasn't just right. But it was close enough. And close enough, Eleanor was learning, was its own kind of just right.

Goodnight to the bears, with their system and their unlocked door.

Goodnight to Baby Bear, who got his eggs.

And goodnight to you — in whatever bed you've found tonight. If it's not quite right, that's okay. You'll find it. Or grow into it. Or build your own.

Goodnight.

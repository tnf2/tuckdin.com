Goldilocks and the Three Bears

Originally by Robert Southey — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 8 min read 📖 2,497 words 🌙 Best read aloud

The bears had a system.

Most families do, whether they know it or not. A set of unspoken agreements about how the morning works, who sits where, what temperature the porridge should be. The bears' system was simple and had been refined over years of quiet cohabitation until it ran like clockwork, or like a household, which is the same thing but messier.

Papa Bear made the porridge. He'd been making it since before Baby Bear was born — the same recipe his mother had used, oats and cream and a pinch of salt that he'd never measured because measuring salt is for people who don't trust their hands. He made it thick. Thick enough that the spoon stood up in it, which was, in his opinion, the only objective measure of porridge quality.

Mama Bear set the table. Three bowls — large, medium, small. Three spoons. Three chairs pushed in at precise angles that no one had agreed on but everyone maintained, because some things in a household are negotiated once and then become law forever. She also opened the kitchen window, which was not about the porridge but about the principle that a kitchen should breathe in the morning.

Baby Bear sat at the table and waited. He was not a baby. He was nine, which is old enough to have opinions and young enough to be ignored when expressing them. He'd asked, several times, if they could have something other than porridge. Eggs, maybe. Or toast with jam. These suggestions had been received with the polite, absolute silence that parents deploy when a request has been heard, considered, and denied without the formality of an actual denial.

The porridge was too hot. It was always too hot. This was not a failure of cooking but a law of physics — porridge retains heat the way grudges retain energy, stubbornly and for longer than seems reasonable. So every morning, the bears went for a walk while it cooled. This was the system. This was how it worked.

They left the door unlocked, because they were bears, and they lived in the woods, and no one locks their door in the woods. This was their first mistake, though calling it a mistake implies they should have known better, and they couldn't have. Trust is only a mistake in retrospect.

Goldilocks was not her real name.

Her real name was Eleanor, but no one had called her that since she was four, when her hair came in so blonde it was nearly white, catching every bit of light like a struck match, and someone said goldilocks and it stuck the way nicknames do — not because they're accurate but because they're easier.

She was nineteen. She was walking through the woods because she'd had an argument with her mother about something that had seemed important at the time and would seem trivial by Thursday. The argument had been loud, and then quiet, and then loud again, and then Eleanor had said something she'd regret and her mother had said something she'd regret and Eleanor had walked out the door because walking out doors is what nineteen-year-olds do when they don't have the language yet for I'm sorry or I'm scared or I don't know who I'm becoming and it terrifies me.

She walked for a long time. The woods were old — the kind of old where the trees had stopped competing with each other and settled into a companionable silence, their canopy so thick that the light below was green and filtered and felt like being underwater. She walked until the anger cooled, which took about forty minutes, and then she walked some more because the walking had become its own thing, a rhythm that didn't require destination.

She found the house the way you find things in the woods: by accident, by wandering, by being lost enough that the world starts showing you things it keeps hidden from people who know where they're going.

It was a cottage. Wooden, well-kept, with window boxes full of flowers she couldn't name and a door that was — she pushed it, just to see — open.

She went in. She shouldn't have. She knew she shouldn't have. But she was nineteen, and the argument was still humming in her chest, and the house was warm and empty and smelled like something cooking, and there's a particular kind of loneliness that makes other people's spaces feel like invitations.

Three bowls of porridge on the table.

She tried the first. Too hot — it burned her tongue, that sharp, papery burn that makes you swear and then feel foolish for swearing at porridge. She tried the second. Too cold — room temperature, basically, which is not a temperature anyone has ever wanted porridge to be. Porridge at room temperature is not food. It's a mistake that hasn't been thrown away yet.

She tried the third. And it was — well. You know.

Just right.

Not a phrase that gets enough credit. Just right. It sounds mediocre. It sounds like settling. But just right is actually the hardest thing to find. Too hot is easy. Too cold is easy. Extremes are everywhere. But the place in the middle — the temperature where you don't have to adjust, where the thing meets you exactly where you are — that's rare. That's the whole endeavor of human existence, really: finding the things that are just right. The job. The person. The city. The way you take your coffee. The pillow. The side of the bed.

She ate the whole bowl.

Three chairs in the living room.

The first was enormous — a papa-sized chair, deep and wide, built for a body much larger than hers. She sank into it and felt immediately lost, the way you feel in a job that's too big for you, or a house with too many rooms. There was space around her on all sides, and none of it felt like hers.

The second was better but wrong in a different way — too soft, too yielding, the kind of chair that accepts you so completely you can't find yourself in it. Like a relationship where the other person agrees with everything you say. Comfortable, technically. But you disappear.

The third chair was small. Small enough that it fit her. Small enough that when she sat in it, her edges touched the edges of the chair, and there was a boundary, and the boundary was right. She leaned back. She sighed. And the chair — a chair built for a nine-year-old bear who wanted eggs for breakfast — held her.

Until it didn't.

The crack was sudden and total. One moment she was resting; the next she was on the floor in a pile of broken wood, startled and sore and surrounded by the wreckage of a thing that had been just right until it wasn't.

That's the problem with just right. Sometimes it can't hold you. Sometimes the thing that fits is also the thing that breaks, because fitting and lasting are not the same quality, and we confuse them constantly.

She went upstairs. She was tired — the walk, the argument, the porridge, the broken chair. Tired in the way that's more than physical. The kind of tired where your body is asking you to please, for the love of God, stop carrying things for a few hours and just lie down.

Three beds.

The first was too hard. The mattress was essentially a suggestion, a thin layer of fabric between her and the unyielding frame, and every bone in her body registered a complaint. There are people who prefer hard beds, and those people are either lying or have different bones.

The second was too soft. She sank into it like a stone into mud, the mattress rising up around her, enveloping her, and instead of comfort she felt claustrophobia. Too much softness is its own kind of trap. You can drown in comfort if there's enough of it.

The third bed was just right.

She didn't think the words. She didn't need to. Her body knew. It relaxed in the way that a fist unclenches — all at once, every muscle letting go simultaneously, a full-body exhale. The pillow was right. The blanket was right. The angle of light from the window was right. Everything was right, and she closed her eyes, and she slept.

She slept the way you sleep when you've been fighting something for a long time and you finally stop. Deep, immediate, dreamless. The kind of sleep that's less like rest and more like surrender.

The bears came home.

They knew immediately. Not because of the porridge or the chair or the rumpled beds — though they noticed all of those — but because the house felt different. A space that's been occupied by a stranger carries the charge of that occupation the way a room carries the echo of a slammed door. Something was here. Something still was.

Papa Bear went first, because that's what Papa Bears do. Up the stairs, down the hall, to the bedroom at the end.

And there she was. Goldilocks. Eleanor. A nineteen-year-old girl with impossible blonde hair, asleep in Baby Bear's bed, curled on her side, one hand tucked under the pillow, looking very much like someone who had been looking for something and had found it in the wrong house.

She woke up.

The bears were there. All three of them, filling the doorway, which is a lot of bear in one doorway. Papa Bear was large in the way that things in nature are large — without effort, without apology. Mama Bear stood slightly behind him, and her expression was the complicated one that mothers have when they're simultaneously angry and curious and something else that might be recognition. Baby Bear was peering between them, nine years old and wide-eyed, staring at the girl in his bed with an expression that said, this is the most interesting thing that has ever happened during breakfast.

Eleanor screamed. Or started to — the scream made it about halfway out before she swallowed it, because the bears weren't moving. They weren't growling. They were just... standing there. Looking at her. The way you look at something unexpected that you're still deciding how to feel about.

"I'm sorry," she said. The words came out before she could think about them, and they were the right words, the ones she'd been unable to find earlier that morning in her mother's kitchen. "I'm so sorry. I shouldn't be here. I was walking, and the door was open, and I was — I don't know what I was doing. I'm sorry about the porridge. And the chair. I broke the chair. I'm sorry."

She was crying. Not dramatically — just leaking, the way people do when the dam breaks and everything that's been held back comes through at once. The argument. The walking. The loneliness that had led her through an open door into a stranger's home, looking for something that was just right in a life that felt too hot and too cold and never, ever the right temperature.

Mama Bear looked at Papa Bear. Something passed between them — not words, because bears don't need words for the things that matter, and neither do people who've been together long enough. It was a look that contained a decision.

Mama Bear walked past Papa Bear, sat on the edge of the bed, and put her paw on Eleanor's shoulder. It was a large paw. Warm. Heavy in a way that was not threatening but grounding, the way a weighted blanket is heavy — pressing you into the present, reminding you that you're here, that you're real, that the floor is solid beneath you even when nothing else feels like it is.

"You ate his porridge," Mama Bear said, nodding toward Baby Bear.

"I know. I'm sorry."

"He's been wanting eggs anyway."

Baby Bear's face lit up. Papa Bear's face did something complicated.

"The chair," Papa Bear said. His voice was deep the way caves are deep — not loud, just resonant.

"I'll fix it," Eleanor said. "Or replace it. I'll—"

"It was too small for him anyway," said Mama Bear. "He's been growing out of it for a year."

They made her tea. She sat at their table — which was too big for her, but not in a bad way, more in the way that sitting at a table with a family that isn't yours is always a little too big, a little unfamiliar, until someone passes you the sugar and suddenly it isn't.

She told them about the argument. Not the details — the details didn't matter — but the shape of it. The feeling of being nineteen and not knowing where you fit. Of trying every chair and every bed and every bowl and finding that nothing was quite right, or that the things that were right broke under the weight of you.

Mama Bear listened. Papa Bear listened. Baby Bear listened and ate eggs, which Papa Bear had made with the resigned grace of a man who knows when a battle is lost.

"Everything is 'just right' for about five minutes," Mama Bear said. "Then you grow, or it shrinks, or the weather changes, and you have to find it again. That's not a problem. That's just life."

Eleanor walked home that afternoon. The woods were the same woods, and the path was the same path, but she was different on it. She went to her mother's kitchen and said the things she'd been unable to say that morning — not perfectly, not eloquently, but honestly, which is better than both.

Her mother listened. And then her mother made porridge, because it was what she had, and they sat together at a table that was not too big and not too small but worn and familiar and theirs, and they ate it.

It wasn't just right.

But it was close enough. And close enough, Eleanor was learning, was its own kind of just right.

Goodnight to the bears, in their house in the woods, with their system and their porridge and their door they still don't lock, because trust is not a mistake just because it was tested.

Goodnight to Baby Bear, who got his eggs.

And goodnight to you, in whatever bed you've found tonight — too hard or too soft or, if you're lucky, just right. And if it's not just right — if nothing is, if the porridge is wrong and the chair is broken and you're sleeping in a bed that doesn't quite fit —

That's okay. You'll find it. Or you'll grow into it. Or you'll build your own.

Goodnight.

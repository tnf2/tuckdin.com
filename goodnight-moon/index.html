Goodnight Moon

Originally by Margaret Wise Brown — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 8 min read 📖 2,055 words 🌙 Best read aloud

There is a room you remember.

Maybe not this exact room — but one like it. A room where the light came in soft and golden in the afternoon, and by evening, everything glowed green and warm, like the inside of a jewel box someone left open just for you.

In this room there was a fireplace, and in the fireplace a fire that had been burning since before you could remember. Not a roaring fire — nothing dramatic. Just a steady, amber thing that popped and murmured to itself the way old fires do when they've been at it long enough to stop showing off.

There was a red balloon tied to the bedpost. No one remembers who put it there. It had simply always been there, drifting in the small currents of warm air, turning slowly like it was thinking something over. Sometimes, if you watched it long enough, you could almost convince yourself it was nodding off too.

And there was a telephone. The old kind, heavy and black, with a cord that curled like a sleeping vine. It hadn't rung in years. It didn't need to. It was there for the same reason the balloon was there — because some things belong in a room, and you don't question them.

A picture hung on the wall of a cow jumping over the moon.

Now, you're old enough to know that cows don't jump over moons. But you're also old enough to know that it doesn't matter. What matters is that someone, a long time ago, painted that cow with such confidence, such joy, that the cow herself seemed to believe it. And if the cow believed it — well. Who were you to argue?

Beside it hung another picture: three bears sitting on chairs. The papa bear was reading a newspaper. The mama bear was knitting something shapeless and green. The baby bear was just sitting there, looking out of the painting with that particular expression baby bears have — the one that says, I know something you don't, and I'm not going to tell you, and that's perfectly fine.

There were two little kittens on the rug.

They were not doing anything useful. Kittens never do. One was batting at a piece of dust that floated in the firelight. The other was curled into a circle so tight and perfect that it seemed less like a kitten and more like a small, furry proof of geometry.

And there were mittens. A pair of them, laid out on the hearth to dry. Red wool, a little pilled, a little stretched at the thumbs from years of being pulled on in a hurry by someone rushing out into the cold. They smelled like snow and cedar and something else — something you can't name but recognize immediately. Something like home and safe and there's nowhere else you need to be.

A little house stood in the corner. A dollhouse, maybe. Or a model. It had tiny windows with tiny curtains and a tiny door that opened if you were patient enough. Inside, there was a tiny table with a tiny chair, and on the tiny table, a tiny bowl of something that might have been porridge. The bears, perhaps, had been consulted during its construction.

There was a young mouse.

She lived behind the baseboard, in a space between the walls that was just big enough for a mouse-sized life. She had a bed made from a matchbox and a cotton ball. She had a thimble she used as a soup pot. She had everything she needed, which wasn't much, because mice understand something that people spend their whole lives trying to learn: enough is not a limitation. Enough is a kind of freedom.

The mouse came out every evening, right around this time. She'd sit by the fire, just at the edge of the rug, and she'd watch the kittens. The kittens never chased her. Some understanding had been reached between them long ago — an unspoken treaty of the hearth. This was the hour when everything was warm and winding down, and there was no room in it for chasing.

And there was a quiet old lady, whispering hush.

She sat in the rocking chair by the window. She had been sitting there for as long as anyone could remember. Her hair was silver, not grey — there's a difference — and her hands rested in her lap the way birds rest on a wire: completely still, but ready.

She didn't read a book. She didn't knit. She just rocked, very slowly, and every now and then she whispered.

Hush.

Not to anyone in particular. Not to the kittens or the mouse or the balloon. She whispered it to the room itself. To the evening. To the way the light was dimming and the fire was settling and the whole world outside was pulling its covers up.

Hush.

And the room listened.

Now.

It was time.

Goodnight room.

Goodnight to the red balloon, still turning, still thinking its slow balloon thoughts. Goodnight to the string that held it, thin as a whisper, strong as a promise.

Goodnight moon.

Goodnight to the moon in the window — the real one, not the one the cow jumped over, though goodnight to that one too. The real moon was full tonight, or close to it, hanging low and heavy like a lantern someone left on for you. It turned the snow on the windowsill to blue silver. It made the whole world look like a photograph of itself, slightly overexposed, slightly dreamlike.

Goodnight cow jumping over the moon. You impossible, wonderful cow. Goodnight to your optimism. Goodnight to whoever looked up at the sky and thought, you know what that needs? A cow. Goodnight to every ridiculous, beautiful thing anyone ever imagined just because they could.

Goodnight light. And the red balloon.

The fire was lower now. The embers pulsed like a slow heartbeat. The balloon's shadow moved across the ceiling in long, lazy arcs. Everything was amber and rust and that particular shade of dark that isn't quite dark yet — the blue hour, some people call it, though it's more of a feeling than a color.

Goodnight bears. Goodnight chairs.

The papa bear had finished his newspaper by now, surely. The mama bear's knitting had grown another shapeless inch. And the baby bear was still looking out of the painting, still knowing his secret, still not telling.

Goodnight kittens. And goodnight mittens.

One kitten had stopped batting at the dust. The dust had won, or maybe the kitten had simply decided that some things aren't worth catching — another lesson it takes people years to learn. The other kitten hadn't moved. The geometry held.

The mittens on the hearth were dry now. Warm all the way through. Tomorrow someone would pull them on again, rush out again into the bright cold morning. But that was tomorrow. Tonight, they rested.

Goodnight clocks. And goodnight socks.

There was a clock on the mantle that ticked so softly you could only hear it when everything else went quiet. And everything else was going quiet now. The ticking wasn't counting anything. It was just keeping time company, the way old friends do — sitting together, not needing to talk.

And the socks. Thick wool ones, draped over the arm of a chair. They were the kind of socks that made you feel like you had your life together, even if you didn't. Even if you were a mess. You put on socks like that and suddenly the world seemed manageable. Warm feet, warm heart. Someone probably cross-stitched that on a pillow once, and they were right.

Goodnight little house. And goodnight mouse.

The mouse had finished her evening by the fire. She'd watched the kittens. She'd felt the warmth on her fur. She'd had a thimble of soup — lentil tonight, if you were curious — and now she was making her way back behind the baseboard, to her matchbox bed and her cotton-ball pillow, to her enough.

Goodnight comb. And goodnight brush.

They sat on the nightstand, side by side. Simple things. Things you use without thinking. But tonight, think about them for a moment. Someone carved that comb. Someone bundled those bristles. Someone made these ordinary objects and sent them out into the world, not knowing they'd end up here, in this room, on this night, being told goodnight by you.

Every object has a journey. Even the small ones. Especially the small ones.

Goodnight nobody.

This is the important one. Goodnight to nobody. Goodnight to the empty chair, the open door, the space in the bed where someone used to be or hasn't been yet. Goodnight to the absence that sometimes sits in the corner of a room like an uninvited guest. You don't have to make it leave. You just have to say goodnight to it, and it becomes a little smaller. A little quieter.

Goodnight nobody. You're welcome here, but you don't get to be loud.

Goodnight mush.

There was a bowl of mush on the nightstand. There's always a bowl of mush. It's the most unglamorous food in the world — just oats and milk and warmth — and yet. And yet. It's the thing you eat when the day has been too much, when you don't have the energy to decide what you want, when all you need is something simple and warm that asks nothing of you.

Goodnight mush. Thank you for being enough.

And goodnight to the old lady whispering hush.

She was still rocking. Still whispering. But slower now, and softer. The whisper had become something less than sound — just breath, just the idea of quiet, just the last small push the evening needed to tip over into night.

Hush.

Goodnight stars. Goodnight air.

Step outside for a moment. In your mind, step outside. Feel the cold — not the bitter kind, just the clean kind, the kind that makes you take a deeper breath. Look up. The stars are absurd. There are too many of them. No one needs that many stars. But there they are, excessive and ancient and completely indifferent to your problems, and somehow that's the most comforting thing in the world.

Your problems are real. But they are also small. The stars don't say this to be cruel. They say it to be kind. Rest, the stars say. We'll keep going. We've been at this for billions of years. We can handle one more night.

Goodnight air. Goodnight to the breath you're taking right now. And the one after it. And the next one after that. You don't have to think about them. They just keep coming. Your body knows what to do. It's been doing it since the moment you arrived, and it will keep doing it while you sleep, quiet and faithful as a clock on a mantle.

Goodnight noises everywhere.

The refrigerator hum. The settling of the house. A car passing outside, its headlights drawing slow arcs across the ceiling. A dog barking, two streets over, at something only dogs find interesting. The radiator clicking. Your own pulse, steady in your ears if you listen for it.

These are the noises of a life. Your life. They're not glamorous. They're not a symphony. But they're yours, and they are playing right now, this very moment, a composition no one else will ever hear exactly this way again.

Goodnight noises everywhere.

The fire is embers now. The balloon has finally stopped turning. The kittens are asleep, the mouse is asleep, and the quiet old lady has stopped rocking. Her eyes are closed. Her hands are still in her lap. She is dreaming, perhaps, of rooms she knew before this one — rooms with their own fires and their own balloons and their own mice behind the baseboard.

The moon is still there, in the window. It will be there when you wake up, or it won't — it has places to be — but right now it's here, and that's what matters.

Right now, everything is here.

The room. The warmth. The quiet.

You.

Goodnight.

Hush.

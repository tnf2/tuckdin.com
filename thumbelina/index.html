Thumbelina

Originally by Hans Christian Andersen — Retold for grown-ups
🕐 9 min read 📖 2,153 words 🌙 Best read aloud

A woman planted a seed because she wanted a child, and what grew was not what she expected.

This is, if you think about it, how all children work. You plant something — a hope, a wish, a decision made at two in the morning or in a doctor's office or in a moment of love or loneliness or both — and what grows is never what you imagined, because imagination is small and life is not.

The seed was from a witch. Not the wicked kind — the neighborhood kind, the kind who lives at the end of the road and knows things about herbs and tides and the ways that wanting bends the world. The woman had been to doctors and wise men and churches, and none of them had helped, and the witch had said, "Plant this. Water it. Wait."

She waited. The seed grew into a flower — a tulip, red and gold, closed tight as a fist. And when the petals opened, curled inside, no bigger than a thumb, was a girl.

The woman named her Thumbelina, because she was the size of a thumb, and because sometimes the most obvious name is the right one. She made her a bed from a walnut shell and a blanket from a rose petal and a boat from a tulip leaf, and Thumbelina floated on a bowl of water and sang songs that the woman could barely hear, tiny and crystalline, like wind chimes made of glass.

She was perfect. She was impossible. She was everything the woman had wanted, at a scale the woman had never imagined.

That's the first lesson: the thing you prayed for might come in a size that doesn't match the picture in your head. Love it anyway.

The toad came at night.

She was a large, wet, ambitious mother toad with a son who needed a wife, and she saw Thumbelina sleeping in her walnut shell and thought: yes. The toad took Thumbelina — walnut shell and all — across the stream and placed her on a lily pad and said, "Stay here. You're going to marry my son."

Thumbelina woke to water on all sides and a toad the size of a dog grinning at her with an expression that was meant to be welcoming but landed somewhere between enthusiasm and hunger.

"I don't want to marry your son," Thumbelina said.

"You'll learn to love him," said the toad, which is a sentence that has never, in the history of sentences, been true.

The toad left. Thumbelina sat on the lily pad, alone, surrounded by water she couldn't cross, in a world she hadn't chosen, waiting for a marriage she didn't want. She cried. Small tears — everything about her was small — but real ones.

The fish heard her. They were minnows, silver and quick, and they'd been watching from below the surface, the way fish watch everything — with curiosity and without judgment. They chewed through the stem of the lily pad. The current took it. Thumbelina floated downstream, away from the toad, away from the son, away from the life someone else had decided she should have.

She floated for a long time. The stream became a river, and the river moved through a world that was enormous and indifferent and beautiful, and Thumbelina sat on her lily pad and watched it all — the dragonflies and the reeds and the sky reflected in the water like a second, upside-down sky — and she felt two things simultaneously: free and lost. Which might be the same feeling, depending on the day.

A beetle found her.

He was a June bug — shiny, black-green, with the self-important buzz of someone who has somewhere to be at all times. He picked her up with his legs and flew her to the top of a tree and showed her to his friends, who were also beetles, and who looked at her with the particular scrutiny of people evaluating something they don't understand.

"She's only got two legs," said one beetle.

"No antennae," said another.

"She's not very pretty," said a third, "is she?"

They said this to each other, not to Thumbelina, which was both better and worse — better because the words weren't aimed at her, worse because they weren't meant for her to overhear, which made them honest, which made them hurt more.

The June bug looked at Thumbelina. He'd thought she was beautiful — minutes ago, on the lily pad, she'd been the most beautiful thing he'd ever seen. But his friends didn't think so. And the gap between what he felt and what they felt was a gap he didn't know how to stand in, so he did what people do: he chose the group.

"Yeah," he said. "Not that pretty."

He left her at the base of the tree.

Thumbelina sat in the grass and thought about the beetles. Not with anger — she wasn't built for anger, which is not the same as saying she couldn't feel it, only that it passed through her quickly, like weather. She thought about how the June bug had looked at her — with wonder, with real admiration — and then how he'd looked at her after his friends spoke — with nothing. How quickly the wonder left. How easily someone else's opinion became his own.

She thought about this for a long time, because she was small, and small things think deeply, and she had nothing else to do.

Summer passed. Autumn came. Thumbelina wandered through a world that was too large for her, eating seeds and drinking dewdrops and sleeping under leaves that were bigger than bedsheets. She was cold. She was hungry. She was alone in the particular way that people are alone when they don't fit — not because no one wants them, but because no one knows what to do with them.

A field mouse found her in November.

She was lying in the stubble of a harvested wheat field, shivering, too cold to move, too proud to have sought shelter, and the field mouse — a plump, practical, warm-hearted creature named Mrs. Fieldmouse — said, "Come inside, child. You'll freeze."

Her home was underground. A burrow — neat, organized, the kind of home that was exactly the right size for its owner and smelled like stored grain and dry earth and safety. Mrs. Fieldmouse gave Thumbelina soup and bread and a bed by the fire, and in return, Thumbelina cleaned and sang, and the arrangement was good, and it was warm, and it was enough.

It should have been enough.

"You should marry Mr. Mole," said Mrs. Fieldmouse.

Mr. Mole was the neighbor. He was wealthy — the underground equivalent of wealthy, which meant extensive tunnels and large grain stores and a black velvet coat that he was very proud of. He was also blind, which Thumbelina didn't mind, and he hated sunshine, which Thumbelina did mind, very much.

"He has a wonderful home," said Mrs. Fieldmouse. "You'd never want for anything."

"I'd want for sunshine."

"Sunshine is overrated."

"Have you ever been in it?"

Mrs. Fieldmouse hadn't. She'd been underground her whole life, and underground was fine, and she genuinely couldn't understand why someone would want to stand in an open field and let light fall on them when there were perfectly good tunnels available. She wasn't cruel. She was limited. There's a difference, though the effect on the person being limited is often the same.

Mr. Mole proposed. The wedding was set for spring. Thumbelina said nothing, because she had nowhere else to go, and having nowhere to go makes cowards of everyone. She agreed to marry Mr. Mole the way people agree to things they don't want: quietly, with the vague hope that something will intervene.

Something intervened.

In the tunnel between Mrs. Fieldmouse's home and Mr. Mole's, Thumbelina found a swallow.

He was nearly dead. He'd fallen — from the sky, through a crack in the earth, into the darkness — and he lay in the tunnel with his wings folded wrong and his heartbeat barely there, a faint, irregular flutter, like a clock running down.

Thumbelina brought him seeds. She brought him water, drop by drop, in a thimble-cap she carried down the tunnel in the dark. She covered him with hay. She lay beside him and pressed her body against his feathers, giving him the only warmth she had, which wasn't much, but was everything.

Night after night. Through the winter. Through the cold.

Mr. Mole passed through the tunnel occasionally and stepped over the bird without interest. "Dead bird," he said. "Someone should clear it out." He didn't look down. He couldn't — he was blind — but even if he could, he wouldn't have. Some people don't look down. Not because they can't see, but because what's down there doesn't interest them.

The swallow recovered. Slowly, then suddenly — the way recovery works, all those quiet nights of care and warmth accumulating until one morning the scale tips and the thing that was dying starts to live.

"Come with me," the swallow said, on the first warm day, when the crack in the tunnel ceiling let in a thread of sunlight that Thumbelina stood in like a person who'd been thirsty for months. "I'm flying south. Come with me."

"I can't fly."

"I can carry you."

"I'm supposed to marry Mr. Mole."

The swallow looked at her. His eyes were black and bright and alive — the eyes of something that had seen the sky from above and the earth from below and knew, with absolute certainty, which one he preferred.

"Do you want to marry Mr. Mole?"

Thumbelina stood in the thread of sunlight. The wedding was in a week. Mrs. Fieldmouse had made a dress. The tunnels were ready. The life was ready — safe, dark, warm, underground, everything accounted for, nothing left to chance.

"No," she said.

It was the first time she'd said it out loud. Not to the toad, who she'd been taken from. Not to the beetles, who she'd been judged by. Not to Mrs. Fieldmouse, who she'd been grateful to. This was the first time she'd said no to a future someone else had chosen for her, out loud, in the sunlight, standing up.

"No," she said again, because it felt so good she wanted to feel it twice.

The swallow carried her south.

She rode on his back, in the curve between his wings, and the world unfolded beneath her — rivers and fields and forests and mountains and the sea, blue and endless and so beautiful that she cried, not from sadness but from the sheer overwhelming fact of it, this world she'd been underground from, this sky she'd been told was overrated.

He brought her to a land where it was always warm. Where flowers grew as tall as houses and the air smelled like honey and the sun — the sun she'd missed, the sun she'd stood in a thread of like a prayer — was everywhere, generous and gold and free.

In the center of a white flower, she found someone her size.

He was a prince — the prince of the flower people, tiny and winged and luminous, and he looked at her the way the June bug had looked at her before his friends spoke: with wonder. But this time, there were no friends to speak. There was no group to choose over her. There was just him, looking at her, seeing her, and saying, with a simplicity that required no translation: you belong here.

They gave her wings.

Transparent, iridescent wings that caught the light and threw small rainbows. She flexed them once, twice, and then she flew — up, into the warm air, through the flowers, into the sky she'd been missing her whole small life.

She flew.

Goodnight to Thumbelina, who was too small for the world until she found the part of the world that was her size.

Goodnight to Mrs. Fieldmouse, who meant well and was wrong. Goodnight to Mr. Mole, who had everything except the ability to look up.

Goodnight to the swallow, who almost died in a tunnel and was saved by someone who had nothing to give except warmth. Which turned out to be exactly enough.

And goodnight to you, if you're underground right now. If the tunnels are safe but the sun is missing. If someone's told you that sunshine is overrated, and you believed them because you had nowhere else to go.

It's not overrated. Go find it. Say no to the mole. Climb on the bird's back. Fly south.

The flower is there. The right size. Waiting.

Goodnight.
